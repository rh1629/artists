ABOUT LÉON BONNAT
========================================
Léon Bonnat was born in Bayonne, in south-west France, on 20 June 1833. From 1846 to 1853 he lived in Madrid, where his father ran a bookshop. There he copied engravings of Old Master paintings, studied painting with José de Madrazo and Federico de Madrazo, and looked closely at Velázquez, Ribera and Titian in the Prado.

In 1854 he moved to Paris, where he studied under Léon Cogniet and also worked in the studio of Paul Delaroche. He won only a second prize in the Prix de Rome, but a grant from Bayonne allowed him to spend about three years in Rome. There he became friends with Edgar Degas, Jean-Jacques Henner and Gustave Moreau. He later travelled to Greece and the Middle East.

Back in Paris he became one of the most sought-after portrait painters of his day, with around 200 portraits of well-known sitters, among them Victor Hugo, Louis Pasteur, Adolphe Thiers, Jules Grévy and Ernest Renan. He won a medal of honour in Paris in 1869 and was elected to the Académie des Beaux-Arts in 1881. He also took on large religious commissions: Saint Vincent de Paul, a success at the Salon of 1866, now hangs in the church of Saint-Nicolas-des-Champs, and The Martyrdom of Saint Denis was painted for the Panthéon.

Bonnat was a respected teacher, first in his own studio and then at the École des Beaux-Arts. His students included painters as different as John Singer Sargent, Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec and Georges Braque. In 1905 he succeeded Paul Dubois as director of the École, a post he held until his death. A keen collector of Old Master drawings, he worked with his friend Antonin Personnaz to create a museum in Bayonne, now the Musée Bonnat-Helleu, which reopened in 2025 after a long renovation. He died at Monchy-Saint-Éloi on 8 September 1922 and was buried in Bayonne.

STYLE AND SUBJECTS
========================================
Bonnat's years in Spain shaped his painting for life. He worked with earthy colours, plain neutral backgrounds and loose, decisive brushwork, and his figures are realistic rather than idealised. The writer Théophile Gautier called him "the antithesis of Bouguereau" because of this stark naturalism and his lack of polished surface finish. Portraits are his most characteristic work, but he also painted religious subjects, Italian peasants and a small number of Orientalist scenes. In later life his handling grew freer and his colour brighter.

Most of our collection is portraits: writers, artists and patrons such as Victor Hugo, Ferdinand de Lesseps and Charles Ephrussi, Barye with a wax model of a seated lion, and Bonnat's own self-portraits. Alongside these are Italian figures such as Roman Girl at a Fountain and Non piangere, Orientalist scenes like An oriental barbershop and Arabian Sheikhs in the Mountains, religious works, etchings of an eagle and of Jacob wrestling with the angel, studies for his decorations in Paris, copies after Michelangelo and Luca Signorelli, and landscapes on the banks of the Tiber.

DECORATING WITH BONNAT PRINTS
========================================
Bonnat's portraits are dark and serious, built on deep browns and blacks, so they suit rooms with a calm, traditional feel: a study, a library or a panelled hallway. A single strong portrait, such as Charles Ephrussi, works well above a desk or a fireplace, and a group of his writers and artists makes a striking gallery wall.

For warmer colour, the Italian and Orientalist scenes bring sunlight and texture. Roman Girl at a Fountain suits a kitchen or dining room, and Arabian Sheikhs in the Mountains a living room or landing. The black-and-white etchings, such as Aigle Liant un Lièvre, are a good choice for smaller spaces like a bedroom, cloakroom or stairwell.

FAQ
========================================
Q: What art movement was Léon Bonnat part of?
A: Bonnat was an academic painter and a member of the Institut de France. His strong naturalism, learned from Spanish painting, placed him between the academic painters and the Impressionists, and one critic even called him a leader of the realist movement.

Q: What is Léon Bonnat known for?
A: He is best known for his portraits of famous people of his time, around 200 in all, including Victor Hugo, Louis Pasteur and Adolphe Thiers. He also painted religious works, Italian genre scenes and a few Orientalist subjects, and he was an influential teacher.

Q: Where can I see Léon Bonnat's original paintings?
A: The Musée Bonnat-Helleu in his home town of Bayonne holds a large collection, including Job. In Paris, the Musée d'Orsay holds Madame Pasca and the Petit Palais holds Christ on the Cross, and the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York has Roman Girl at a Fountain.

Q: How can I recognise Léon Bonnat's style?
A: Look for dark, earthy colours, plain neutral backgrounds and loose, decisive brushwork. His figures are realistic rather than idealised, with little surface polish, in the manner of the Spanish painters he admired.

Q: Who were Léon Bonnat's students?
A: His many students included John Singer Sargent, Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec, Gustave Caillebotte, Thomas Eakins, Edvard Munch, Raoul Dufy and Georges Braque. He encouraged them to visit the Prado in Madrid to study Spanish painting.
