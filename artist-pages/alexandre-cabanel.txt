ABOUT ALEXANDRE CABANEL
========================================
Alexandre Cabanel was born in Montpellier on 28 September 1823, the son of a carpenter of modest means, and died in Paris on 23 January 1889. He began his training at the Montpellier School of Fine Arts, in the class of Charles Matet, curator of the Musée Fabre. A grant took him to Paris in 1839, and in 1840 he entered the École des Beaux-Arts, where he studied with François-Édouard Picot.

After two unsuccessful attempts, with Cincinnatus Receiving the Ambassadors of Rome in 1843 and Christ in the Garden of Olives in 1844, he was placed in the Prix de Rome in 1845 and lived at the Villa Medici in Rome until 1850. In Montpellier he painted portraits of members of wealthy families, such as the Marès family. He moved over the years towards romantic themes, and in 1855 he was made a knight of the Legion of Honour.

Fame came with The Birth of Venus, shown at the Salon of 1863 and bought straight away by Napoleon III for his own collection. That year Cabanel was elected to the Académie des Beaux-Arts, and in 1864 he became a professor at the École des Beaux-Arts, where he taught until his death. His studio trained a great many painters, among them Jules Bastien-Lepage, Henri Gervex and Aristide Maillol. He sat on the Salon jury seventeen times between 1868 and 1888 and won the Salon's medal of honour in 1865, 1867 and 1878. As a juror he opposed new movements, and writers such as Émile Zola and Joris-Karl Huysmans criticised him, yet in 1881 he spoke up for Édouard Manet's portrait of the lion hunter Pertuiset.

He also painted large decorative schemes, including rooms in the Pereire brothers' mansion in Paris and the Life of Saint Louis panels for the Panthéon, shown at the 1878 Exposition Universelle. European celebrities and American collectors commissioned portraits from him. He was made a commander of the Legion of Honour in 1884, and after his death in Paris he was buried in Montpellier. His work is held by the Musée Fabre in Montpellier, which gave him his first retrospective in 2010, the Musée d'Orsay in Paris, the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York and the Royal Museum of Fine Arts in Antwerp.

========================================
STYLE AND SUBJECTS
========================================
Cabanel painted historical, classical and religious subjects in the academic style: graceful modelling, smooth, silky brushwork and idealised figures. Many of his best-known pictures centre on a single figure from myth, the Bible or literature, such as Venus, Echo or the fallen angel, and some, like Albaydé, take their romantic subjects from poetry. He was just as well known for portraits, and his sitters ranged from Napoleon III to European aristocrats and wealthy Americans.

The collection reflects that range. There are mythological figures such as Echo, Pandora, Psyché and Vénus, and biblical and historical scenes from The Death of Moses and The Expulsion of Adam and Eve to Cleopatra and Phèdre, as well as panels from his Life of Saint Louis for the Panthéon. Portraits include Napoleon III, the opera singer Christina Nilsson as Ophelia, Catharine Lorillard Wolfe and Mary Victoria Leiter, later Lady Curzon. A group of drawings, drapery studies and rocky landscapes shows the working side of his studio.

DECORATING WITH CABANEL PRINTS
========================================
Cabanel's single-figure pictures, with their pale skin tones, soft blues and creams, suit a bedroom or a quiet dressing room. The Birth of Venus and Echo bring calm rather than grandeur, and Albaydé, with its cushions and greenery, works well above a bed or beside a reading chair.

The large story paintings have more drama. Cleopatra Testing Poisons on Condemned Prisoners or Phèdre makes a strong focal point in a living room, dining room or study, and the Saint Louis panels, such as Saint Louis rendant la justice, hang well as a set along a hallway or staircase. For a formal, classic look, hang portraits such as Portrait of Miss Fanny Clapp and Petrarch's Laura together in a dining room or entrance hall.

FAQ
========================================
Q: What art movement was Alexandre Cabanel part of?
A: Cabanel was an academic painter, one of the leading figures of French academic art under the Second Empire. He trained at the École des Beaux-Arts, was elected to the Académie des Beaux-Arts in 1863 and taught at the École from 1864 until his death.

Q: What is Alexandre Cabanel known for?
A: He is best known for The Birth of Venus, shown at the Salon of 1863 and bought by Napoleon III. He painted historical, classical and religious subjects, was a much sought-after portrait painter, and taught a great many students at the École des Beaux-Arts.

Q: Where can I see Alexandre Cabanel's original works?
A: The Musée Fabre in his home town of Montpellier holds many of his works, including The Fallen Angel, Albaydé and Phèdre. The Birth of Venus is in the Musée d'Orsay in Paris and his Life of Saint Louis is in the Panthéon, while other works are in the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York and the Royal Museum of Fine Arts in Antwerp.

Q: How can I recognise Alexandre Cabanel's style?
A: Look for smooth, polished surfaces, gentle modelling and idealised figures, the marks of academic art. His subjects come from myth, the Bible and history, often built around one graceful figure, alongside formal portraits.

Q: Was Pierre Cabanel related to Alexandre Cabanel?
A: Yes. The painter Pierre Cabanel was his nephew and one of his pupils. Alexandre painted his portrait in 1883, and it is now in the Musée Fabre in Montpellier.
