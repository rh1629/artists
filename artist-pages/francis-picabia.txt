========================================
ABOUT FRANCIS PICABIA
========================================
Francis Picabia was born in Paris on 22 January 1879, to a French mother and a Cuban father of Spanish descent. His mother died when he was a young boy, and he was raised by his father. In the 1890s he studied at the École des Arts Décoratifs in Paris under Fernand Cormon, Ferdinand Humbert and Albert Charles Wallet. An inheritance from his mother later left him far wealthier than most artists of his time.

His first success came with Impressionist landscapes. Influenced by Alfred Sisley, he painted riverbanks, lanes, churches and washhouses around Moret-sur-Loing, as well as the south at Martigues and Saint-Tropez, often working in the studio from photographs and postcards. His first solo exhibition, at the Galerie Haussmann in Paris in 1905, was a triumph. In 1909 he married Gabriële Buffet, who encouraged his break with Impressionism. Around 1911 he joined the Puteaux Group, where he became friends with Marcel Duchamp and Guillaume Apollinaire, and in 1913 he was the only artist from the Cubist circle to attend the Armory Show in New York in person.

During the First World War he moved between New York, Barcelona and Switzerland. From 1915 he made drawings of strange machines, which he used as mock portraits of people and feelings. In January 1917 he launched the magazine 391 in Barcelona, and he became one of the leading figures of Dada in New York, Zürich and Paris, before breaking with the movement in 1921. In 1924 he wrote the scenario for René Clair's short film Entr'acte and designed the sets for the ballet Relâche, with music by Erik Satie.

From 1925 he lived in Mougins, on the Côte d'Azur, and returned to figurative painting, first with garish "Monster" pictures and then with his "Transparencies", which layer one image over another. In 1940 he married Olga Mohler and spent the war years in the south of France, painting nudes and portraits from magazine photographs. He returned to Paris after the war, went back to abstract painting and wrote poetry. A retrospective was held at the Galerie René Drouin in 1949. He died in Paris on 30 November 1953 and is buried in Montmartre Cemetery. His work is held by the Museum of Modern Art and the Guggenheim in New York, the Philadelphia Museum of Art, the Art Institute of Chicago, Tate in London and the Centre Pompidou in Paris.

========================================
STYLE AND SUBJECTS
========================================
Picabia never settled on one style. "If you want to have clean ideas, change them like shirts," he wrote, and his work moves from soft, sunlit Impressionism to bright abstract planes, then to cool mechanical diagrams with handwritten words, thick and garish figures, overlapping transparent drawings, glossy photo-based portraits and, at the end, simple abstract marks. What links them is a sense of play and a love of provoking the viewer.

The collection reflects that range. There are Impressionist river and village scenes from Moret, Montigny and Villeneuve-sur-Yonne, olive trees and boats at Martigues, and a sunlit Notre-Dame. Machine drawings such as Prostitution universelle and Petite solitude au milieu des soleils sit alongside abstract works like Animation, layered line drawings such as Transparence, and a series of Spanish women. Later pieces include the clown and nudes of the early 1940s, bright flower and bird paintings, and the dark, ghostly Mains et fantômes from 1948.

DECORATING WITH PICABIA PRINTS
========================================
Picabia's Impressionist landscapes, full of blue water, green trees and warm southern light, suit a living room, bedroom or hallway where you want something calm and bright. Les laveuses à Moret and Les oliviers, effet de soleil aux Martigues work well as a pair, and the river views from Moret and Montigny make an easy group for a staircase wall.

For a more modern room, the machine drawings bring wit to a study or home office: Petite solitude au milieu des soleils, with its yellow and orange mechanism, looks sharp in a simple frame. The colourful later paintings, such as Geai bleu with its stylised bird on deep blue, or the pansies of Fleurs on black, give a kitchen, dining room or bold living space a strong focal point.

FAQ
========================================
Q: What art movement was Francis Picabia part of?
A: Picabia is most closely associated with Dada, and was one of its early major figures in the United States and France. Before that he painted in Impressionist and Cubist styles, and he was later briefly associated with Surrealism.

Q: What is Francis Picabia known for?
A: He is known for changing style many times, for his machine drawings of the Dada years, and for his magazine 391. He also worked as a poet and designed the ballet Relâche, with its film Entr'acte.

Q: Where can I see Francis Picabia's original works?
A: Public collections with his work include the Museum of Modern Art and the Guggenheim in New York, the Philadelphia Museum of Art, the Art Institute of Chicago, Tate in London and the Centre Pompidou in Paris.

Q: Did Francis Picabia paint landscapes?
A: Yes. From about 1903 to 1908 he painted Impressionist landscapes influenced by Alfred Sisley, including riverbanks, lanes, churches and washhouses. They were popular, and his first solo exhibition in Paris in 1905 was a success.

Q: How can I recognise Picabia's style?
A: Because his style changed so often, look for his themes rather than one manner: mechanical diagrams with written words, figures drawn over one another in his Transparencies, Spanish women, and, in his early work, sunlit French rivers.
