ABOUT TADEUSZ MAKOWSKI
========================================
Tadeusz Makowski was born in Oświęcim on 29 January 1882, the son of a railway official. From 1902 to 1906 he studied classical philology and Polish at the Jagiellonian University in Kraków. At the same time he trained as a painter at the Kraków Academy of Fine Arts, where his teachers were Józef Unierzyski, Jan Stanisławski and Józef Mehoffer. He finished there in 1908 with a diploma and a silver medal.

Around 1908 he moved to Paris, where he lived for the rest of his life. His early work followed the manner of his teachers, and in Paris he was at first drawn to the murals of Pierre Puvis de Chavannes. In about 1910 he became friends with Henri Le Fauconnier and, through him, met the Cubist painters of Montparnasse. Under their influence he simplified his forms and made them more geometric. In 1912 he began a diary that he kept until 1931.

During the First World War he spent time in Brittany at the invitation of the painter Władysław Ślewiński, and he went back there several times. Brittany turned him away from strict Cubism and back to painting from nature. In 1922 he spent the summer in the Auvergne, where he painted Children's Band, the work that marks the start of his personal style. From 1923 he showed at the Galerie Berthe Weill in Paris with an international group of painters that included Marcel Gromaire and Jules Pascin, and he held solo exhibitions there in 1927 and 1928. He also lived briefly in the Netherlands in the 1920s and was a member of the Society of Polish Artists in Paris.

Makowski died in Paris on 1 November 1932 and is buried at Les Champeaux cemetery in Montmorency. In 1954 a large part of his work returned to Poland and went to the National Museum in Warsaw, which held a monographic exhibition of his painting in 1960. His diary was published in Warsaw the following year. His work is also held by the national museums in Kraków, Poznań and Wrocław and by the Silesian Museum in Katowice.

========================================
STYLE AND SUBJECTS
========================================
Makowski built a personal style from Cubism, old Dutch and Netherlandish painting, Polish folk art and naive realism. His figures are made of simple shapes, spheres, cones and blocks, so that they look like puppets carved from wood, yet they keep very human looks and gestures. Children are his favourite subject: small figures in pointed caps, playing music, dressing up for carnival, putting on plays or going about village life. He also painted landscapes with figures, still lifes and portraits, and made woodcut book illustrations.

The collection follows his whole career. Works dated 1906 to 1908 include moonlit landscapes and stage designs, and a group from 1913 and 1914 shows Cubist-influenced nudes, still lifes and street views. Later come Breton landscapes, views of Espaly and Le Puy dated 1920 and 1921, a summer in Normandy and the lighthouse at Tréport. A set of drawings from 1924 are designs for illustrations to Pastorałki, a book of Polish Christmas poems, and the largest part of the collection is the children, carnival and village scenes and the many portrait heads of children from his last decade.

FAQ
========================================
Q: What art movement was Tadeusz Makowski part of?
A: Makowski belongs to the School of Paris, the circle of artists from many countries who worked in Paris in the early 20th century. Cubism shaped his early Paris work, and his mature style also draws on Polish folk art, Dutch painting and naive realism.

Q: What is Tadeusz Makowski known for?
A: He is best known for his paintings of children, shown as small, doll-like figures in village, theatre, music and carnival scenes. Children's Band (1922) is seen as the start of his personal style, and The Shoemaker (1930) as one of its high points.

Q: Where can I see Tadeusz Makowski's original works?
A: The National Museum in Warsaw holds a large group of his paintings, including Children's Band, Jazz and The Shoemaker. Other works are in the national museums in Kraków, Poznań and Wrocław and in the Silesian Museum in Katowice.

Q: How can I recognise Tadeusz Makowski's style?
A: Look for small figures, often children in pointed caps, built from simple geometric shapes so that they resemble wooden puppets. The mood is quiet and lyrical, and the subjects are everyday: village life, music, carnival and play.

Q: Did Tadeusz Makowski keep a diary?
A: Yes. He kept a diary from 1912 to 1931, and it was published in Warsaw in 1961.
