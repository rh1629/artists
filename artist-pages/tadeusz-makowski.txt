========================================
ABOUT TADEUSZ MAKOWSKI
========================================
Tadeusz Makowski was born in Oświęcim, in southern Poland, on 29 January 1882, the son of a railway official. From 1902 to 1906 he studied classical philology and Polish at the Jagiellonian University in Kraków. At the same time he trained at the Kraków Academy of Fine Arts, where his teachers were Józef Unierzyski, Jan Stanisławski and Józef Mehoffer. He finished his studies there in 1908.

In 1908 he moved to Paris, which remained his home for the rest of his life. He admired the murals of Pierre Puvis de Chavannes, and his own frescoes caught the attention of a group of Cubist painters in Montparnasse led by Henri Le Fauconnier. This contact drew him into Cubism.

When the First World War began, the painter Władysław Ślewiński invited him to Brittany. He spent the war years there and returned several times afterwards, and it was there that he turned from strict Cubism back to painting from nature. He also lived briefly in the Netherlands in the 1920s. From 1923 he exhibited with an international group of painters, including Marcel Gromaire and Jules Pascin, at the Galerie Berthe Weill in Paris, which gave him solo shows in 1927 and 1928.

Makowski died in Paris on 1 November 1932 and was buried at Montmorency, north of the city. He kept a diary from 1912 to 1931, which was published in Warsaw in 1961. In 1954 a large part of his work went to the National Museum in Warsaw, which holds the largest collection of his art. The National Museums in Kraków, Wrocław and Poznań and the Silesian Museum in Katowice also hold his paintings.

========================================
STYLE AND SUBJECTS
========================================
Makowski built a personal, poetic style from many sources: Cubism, Dutch and Flemish painting, Polish folk art and the naive painters of his own day. His forms are simplified and partly geometric. Children appear as small figures in pointed caps, with something of the stiff charm of wooden puppets, in quiet village and home scenes, in theatre and music making, and in masquerades and carnivals. He also looked at folk paper cut-outs, glass painting from the Podhale region and French popular prints, and he made woodcuts and engravings, including book illustrations.

The collection follows his whole career. Early works from 1906 to 1909 include moonlit landscapes, stage designs and religious scenes, while works dated 1913 and 1914 show his Cubist side in Paris street views, nudes and still lifes. Landscapes titled after Espaly and Le Puy (1920–21), Normandy (1926) and Tréport (1927) record time in the French countryside and on the coast. Most of all there are children: bands, carnivals, beaches, backyards and masquerades from the 1920s and early 1930s, alongside portraits, flower pieces and his 1924 designs for Pastorałki, a book of Polish Christmas carols.

DECORATING WITH MAKOWSKI PRINTS

Makowski's children's scenes are cheerful without being sugary, which makes them a natural choice for a nursery, a child's bedroom or a family kitchen. Scenes such as Children going fishing, with its browns and ochres, look good hung in a group along a hallway or staircase, where the little figures seem to chat from frame to frame.

FAQ
========================================
Q: What art movement was Tadeusz Makowski part of?
A: Makowski is counted among the painters of the École de Paris (School of Paris). He was drawn into Cubism in his early Paris years, then developed a personal style that drew on folk art, Dutch painting and naive realism.

Q: What is Tadeusz Makowski known for?
A: He is best known for his paintings of children: small figures in pointed caps making music, dressing up for carnival and playing out little dramas. He also painted French landscapes, portraits and still lifes, and made woodcuts and engravings.

Q: Where can I see Tadeusz Makowski's original works?
A: The National Museum in Warsaw holds the largest collection of his work and shows his paintings in its 20th-century galleries. The National Museums in Kraków, Wrocław and Poznań, the Silesian Museum in Katowice and the Musée National d'Art Moderne in Paris also hold his work.

Q: How can I recognise Tadeusz Makowski's style?
A: Look for doll-like children in pointed caps, set in simple scenes built from geometric shapes. The mood is quiet and lyrical; the critic Ignacy Witz said each of his paintings is like a poem.

Q: Did Tadeusz Makowski live in France?
A: Yes. He moved to Paris in 1908 and lived there until his death in 1932, spending the First World War in Brittany and returning there several times.
