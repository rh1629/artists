========================================
ABOUT JOHN HOPPNER
========================================
John Hoppner was born in Whitechapel, London, in 1758, to German parents. His mother was one of the German attendants at the royal palace, and George III took a fatherly interest in the boy. Hoppner sang as a chorister in the royal chapel, and the king gave him a small allowance to study painting.

He entered the Royal Academy Schools in 1775. He won a silver medal for drawing from life in 1778 and the Academy's gold medal in 1782 for a scene from King Lear. He first exhibited at the Academy in 1780, and in 1782 he married Phoebe Wright, daughter of the American-born sculptor Patience Wright. Their sons included Richard Belgrave Hoppner, later British consul general in Venice and a friend of Byron, and Henry Parkyns Hoppner, a naval officer and Arctic explorer.

Hoppner began as a landscape painter but turned to portraits to earn a living, and he soon had the most fashionable sitters of the day. He became portrait painter to the Prince of Wales, the future George IV, who often visited his studio. He was elected an Associate of the Royal Academy in the early 1790s and a full Academician in 1795. His sitters included Lord Nelson, the Duke of Wellington, Walter Scott and Sir George Beaumont, and his great rival was the younger Thomas Lawrence.

In his later years Hoppner suffered from a long illness, and he died in London on 23 January 1810. His work is held in the Royal Collection, the National Gallery in London, the Wallace Collection, the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York and the National Gallery of Art in Washington.

========================================
STYLE AND SUBJECTS
========================================
Hoppner was seen as a close follower of Sir Joshua Reynolds, and critics praised him above all as a colourist. His portraits are softer and more natural than Lawrence's, especially those of women and children, which are often counted among his best work. His early love of landscape shows in the open-air backgrounds of his portraits and in his chalk drawings of trees and rocks. He rarely painted history or mythology, but a handful of classical subjects survive, among them a Bacchante and Jupiter and Io.

The collection reflects this range. There are society women such as Lady Cunliffe and Susannah Edith, Lady Rowley; officers and public men such as Major Thomas Pechell and Thomas Erskine, Lord Chancellor; and the actresses Mrs. Jordan and Mrs. Abington. Children appear in family groups and on their own, from The Bowden Children to The Little Gardener and Girl with a Rabbit. Classical subjects and landscape drawings, such as A Rocky Bank and Study of a Massive Rock, complete the picture.

DECORATING WITH HOPPNER PRINTS
========================================
Hoppner's portraits of children have a gentle, everyday charm that suits a nursery, a child's bedroom or a family living room. The Little Gardener or Girl with a Rabbit work well on their own, while Cecilia, a girl in a black cape and white bonnet, adds a quiet note of contrast to a pale wall.

For a hallway, study or dining room, his formal portraits bring a sense of history. An Unknown British Officer, in scarlet and gold, makes a strong single piece above a fireplace or console table. His monochrome landscape drawings, such as A Rocky Bank, are subtle enough for a reading corner or a study and sit well in a group of small frames.

FAQ
========================================
Q: What art movement was John Hoppner part of?
A: Hoppner belonged to the Georgian school of British portrait painting, in the tradition of Sir Joshua Reynolds. He was a member of the Royal Academy, elected a full Academician in 1795.

Q: What is John Hoppner known for?
A: He is known for fashionable portraits of Georgian society, especially women and children, painted with rich colour. He was portrait painter to the Prince of Wales and the main rival of Thomas Lawrence.

Q: Where can I see John Hoppner's original works?
A: His paintings are in the Royal Collection, the National Gallery and the Wallace Collection in London, the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York, and the National Gallery of Art in Washington, which holds The Frankland Sisters.

Q: How can I recognise John Hoppner's style?
A: Look for warm, rich colour, relaxed poses and soft, open-air landscape backgrounds. His women and children look natural rather than posed, which set him apart from the more glamorous Lawrence.

Q: Was John Hoppner the son of George III?
A: There is no evidence for it. His mother worked at the royal palace and the king took an interest in him, which led to gossip that Hoppner is said to have encouraged, but the rumours are considered unfounded.
