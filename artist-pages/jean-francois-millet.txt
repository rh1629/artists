ABOUT JEAN-FRANÇOIS MILLET
========================================
Jean-François Millet was born on 4 October 1814 in Gruchy, a farming hamlet in Gréville on the Normandy coast, and died in Barbizon on 20 January 1875. He was the eldest son of a farming family. Village priests taught him Latin and introduced him to books, but he also worked on the family farm: mowing, making hay, threshing, ploughing and sowing. These tasks returned again and again in his art.

His father sent him to Cherbourg to train with a local portrait painter, Du Mouchel, and then with Théophile Langlois de Chèvreville. A grant took him to Paris in 1837, where he studied at the École des Beaux-Arts under Paul Delaroche. When the grant ended in 1839, he made his living painting portraits in Cherbourg, Le Havre and Paris. His first wife, Pauline Ono, died in 1844. The following year he moved to Le Havre with Catherine Lemaire, whom he married in 1853. They had nine children.

In Paris in the mid-1840s he became friends with Théodore Rousseau, Narcisse Diaz, Constant Troyon and Charles Jacque, and Honoré Daumier's figure drawing shaped the way he drew peasants. In 1848 the government bought his painting The Winnower. In 1849 he settled in the village of Barbizon with Catherine and their children, and he lived there for the rest of his life. There he painted The Sower, The Gleaners and The Angelus, the three pictures that made his name, and became one of the founders of the Barbizon School.

His reputation grew through the 1860s. The 1867 Exposition Universelle in Paris showed a large group of his paintings, and in 1868 he was made a Chevalier of the Legion of Honour. During the Franco-Prussian War the family took refuge in Cherbourg and Gréville, returning to Barbizon late in 1871. His Barbizon house became a museum in 1923. The largest collections of his work are at the Musée d'Orsay in Paris, the Museum of Fine Arts in Boston, the Musée Thomas-Henry in Cherbourg and the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York.

STYLE AND SUBJECTS
========================================
Millet painted the farm work he had known since childhood: sowing, digging, gleaning, planting potatoes, haymaking and minding sheep. His figures are solid and heavy, often bent towards the ground, and set against low horizons and wide evening skies. Colours are earthy, with dark homespun clothes against golden fields. In the 1850s he also painted quiet cottage interiors, with women caring for children or busy with household work. Late in life he turned more to pure landscape, paying close attention to light, dusk and shadow. He worked in oil, pastel, Conté crayon and etching.

The collection follows this whole range. The great field scenes, including The Angelus, The Gleaners and Potato Planters, sit alongside his etched version of The Gleaners from the mid-1850s. Shepherdesses and their flocks appear in paintings, drawings and prints. Cliffs and hamlets around Gréville, views near Vichy and in the Auvergne, and the night sky of Starry Night show the landscape painter. Early portraits, such as Louise-Antoinette Feuardent, and many working drawings complete the picture.

DECORATING WITH MILLET PRINTS
========================================
Millet's field scenes, with their golden light and earthy browns and greens, suit a living room, dining room or farmhouse-style kitchen. The Angelus works well on its own as a quiet centrepiece, while the wide format of Haystacks: Autumn fits the space above a sofa, sideboard or fireplace.

His cottage interiors are more intimate. Woman Sewing by Lamplight, with its warm glow in a dark room, suits a bedroom, a reading corner or a snug, and Starry Night is a calm, unusual choice for a bedroom. For a hallway, study or staircase, his etchings and drawings of shepherdesses and field workers, in soft browns and greys, hang well together as a group.

FAQ
========================================
Q: What art movement was Jean-François Millet part of?
A: Millet belongs to the Realism movement and was one of the founders of the Barbizon School, named after the village where he lived from 1849. His friends in the group included Théodore Rousseau, Narcisse Diaz and Constant Troyon.

Q: What is Jean-François Millet known for?
A: He is best known for his paintings of peasant farmers at work, above all The Sower, The Gleaners and The Angelus. He is also noted for his pastels, Conté crayon drawings and etchings, and for the landscapes of his later years.

Q: Where can I see Jean-François Millet's original works?
A: The largest collections are at the Musée d'Orsay in Paris, which holds The Gleaners and The Angelus, the Museum of Fine Arts in Boston, the Musée Thomas-Henry in Cherbourg and the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York. His house in Barbizon has been a museum since 1923.

Q: How can I recognise a Millet painting?
A: Look for solid, heavy figures absorbed in farm work, often bent towards the ground, set against a low horizon and a wide sky. The colours are mostly earthy browns, greens and golds, and the mood is calm and serious.

Q: Did Millet influence Vincent van Gogh?
A: Yes. Millet was an important inspiration for Van Gogh, especially early in his career, and Van Gogh mentions him many times in letters to his brother Theo. He also made his own versions of some of Millet's rural scenes.
