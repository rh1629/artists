========================================
ABOUT GUSTAVE CAILLEBOTTE
========================================
Gustave Caillebotte was born in Paris on 19 August 1848 into a wealthy family. His father ran the family's military textile business. Gustave earned a law degree, and during the Franco-Prussian War he served in the Garde Nationale Mobile de la Seine from 1870 to 1871. Summers were spent at the family estate at Yerres, south-east of Paris, which his father had bought in 1860.

After the war he studied painting in the studio of Léon Bonnat. He entered the École des Beaux-Arts in 1873 but stayed only about a year. Through his friend, the painter Giuseppe De Nittis, he met Edgar Degas. His father died in 1874, and the family fortune meant Caillebotte never had to sell his work. In 1875 the Salon turned down The Floor Scrapers, a painting of workmen preparing a wooden floor. The next year he showed it at the Second Impressionist Exhibition, and by 1877 he was organising and helping to pay for the group's shows. He took part in five of the eight Impressionist exhibitions.

He also bought works by his friends, including Claude Monet, Pierre-Auguste Renoir and Camille Pissarro, and at least once paid the rent on Monet's studio. After his mother died in 1878, the Yerres estate was sold, and he moved with his brother Martial to an apartment on the Boulevard Haussmann. In 1881 the brothers bought a property at Petit-Gennevilliers, on the Seine near Argenteuil, and Gustave settled there for good in 1888. He painted less and turned to gardening and to designing and racing yachts. Renoir was a regular visitor, and Caillebotte was one of the models in Renoir's Luncheon of the Boating Party. Caillebotte died on 21 February 1894, aged 45, while working in his garden.

He left his collection of paintings by his Impressionist friends to the French state. After long talks with Renoir, who was his executor, 38 of them went on show at the Musée du Luxembourg in 1897, the first public display of the Impressionists in France. His own painting was overlooked for decades, until the Art Institute of Chicago bought Paris Street; Rainy Day in 1964 and interest grew from the 1970s. His work is held today by the Musée d'Orsay in Paris, the Art Institute of Chicago, the J. Paul Getty Museum and many other museums.

========================================
STYLE AND SUBJECTS
========================================
Caillebotte wanted to paint life as he saw it, and his work is more realistic than that of most Impressionists. He is known for steep, plunging perspective: streets seen from a balcony, rooftops seen from above, bridges whose iron girders cut across the picture, and figures cropped at the edge of the frame. His Paris scenes are smooth and almost photographic, while his country and river pictures use a softer, looser touch. He painted the new boulevards of Paris, quiet family interiors and portraits, working men, rowers and sailing boats, still lifes of food and, in his later years, flowers and gardens.

The collection follows him through all of these. There are the Paris scenes, such as Paris Street; Rainy Day, Le Pont de l'Europe and Young Man at His Window, and family pictures from Yerres such as The Orange Trees. River views from Argenteuil and Petit-Gennevilliers show sailing boats, bridges and banks of the Seine, and there are seaside villas and gardens at Trouville. Flower pictures from his own garden include chrysanthemums, dahlias and roses. There are also still lifes, portraits of friends such as Henri Cordier, an early canal view near Naples and two pencil studies of men.

DECORATING WITH CAILLEBOTTE PRINTS
========================================
Caillebotte's Paris scenes, in cool greys and blues with strong straight lines, suit a hallway, a study or a home office, where their calm order works well. Rooftops in the Snow and Le Pont de l'Europe make a good pair, and Paris Street; Rainy Day is large and detailed enough to anchor a living room wall on its own.

For warmth and light, turn to his river and garden pictures. Skiffs, with its yellow oars on green water, brings summer to a living room or a bathroom, and Chemin montant, a couple walking a sunny garden path under an orange parasol, suits a bedroom or a garden room. His still lifes, such as Fruit Displayed on a Stand, are a natural fit for a kitchen or dining room.

FAQ
========================================
Q: What art movement was Gustave Caillebotte part of?
A: Caillebotte was a member of the Impressionists and showed work in five of their eight exhibitions. He painted in a more realistic manner than most of the group, so he is also linked with Realism.

Q: What is Gustave Caillebotte known for?
A: He is best known for his paintings of modern Paris, such as Paris Street; Rainy Day and Le Pont de l'Europe, with their bold, unusual perspective. He is also known as a patron of the Impressionists, whose work he bought and left to the French state.

Q: Where can I see Gustave Caillebotte's original works?
A: Paris Street; Rainy Day is in the Art Institute of Chicago, and the Musée d'Orsay in Paris holds The Floor Scrapers and Boating Party. Young Man at His Window is at the J. Paul Getty Museum in Los Angeles, and The Orange Trees is at the Museum of Fine Arts, Houston.

Q: How can I recognise Caillebotte's style?
A: Look for steep, plunging perspective and high viewpoints: streets seen from balconies, bridges and pavements running sharply into the distance, and figures cut off at the edge of the picture. His city scenes are smooth and almost photographic, while his river and garden scenes are looser and brighter.

Q: Why is Caillebotte important to the history of Impressionism?
A: He helped organise and pay for the Impressionist exhibitions and bought his friends' paintings when few others would. He left them to the French state, and their display at the Musée du Luxembourg in 1897 was the first public showing of the Impressionists in France.
