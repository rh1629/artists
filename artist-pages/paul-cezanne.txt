ABOUT PAUL CÉZANNE
========================================
Paul Cézanne was born in Aix-en-Provence on 19 January 1839. His father co-founded a bank in the town, which later gave the painter a security few of his fellow artists had. At school in Aix he became close friends with the future novelist Émile Zola. He took drawing classes in Aix from 1857 and, at his father's wish, began studying law, but in 1861 he left for Paris to paint.

In Paris he was turned down by the École des Beaux-Arts and worked instead at the Académie Suisse, where he met Camille Pissarro. His work was shown at the Salon des Refusés in 1863, but the official Salon rejected it year after year. In the early 1870s he painted beside Pissarro at Pontoise and Auvers-sur-Oise, lightened his palette, and showed with the Impressionists at their first exhibition in 1874 and again in 1877. Hortense Fiquet, whom he met in 1869 and married in 1886, became the person he painted most often.

From the early 1880s he lived mostly in Provence, painting at the family estate of the Jas de Bouffan, at L'Estaque on the coast near Marseille, and in the countryside around Mont Sainte-Victoire. His father's death in 1886 left him financially secure. Recognition came slowly: the dealer Ambroise Vollard gave him his first solo exhibition in Paris in 1895, and younger artists began to seek him out.

In 1902 he had a studio built on the Chemin des Lauves, just north of Aix, where he worked on his large bather paintings. In October 1906 he was caught in a storm while painting outdoors, fell ill, and died in Aix on 22 October 1906. Matisse and Picasso are both said to have called him "the father of us all". His work is held by museums around the world, including the Musée d'Orsay in Paris, the Musée Granet in Aix, the National Gallery in London, the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York and the Philadelphia Museum of Art.

STYLE AND SUBJECTS
========================================
Cézanne built his pictures from small, repeated brushstrokes and planes of colour, so that fruit, rocks and hillsides feel solid and carefully constructed. He said he wanted to make of Impressionism "something solid and lasting like the art in the museums". In his still lifes he arranged fruit, jugs and white tablecloths in the studio and changed sizes and angles for the sake of balance. In his landscapes, houses, trees and mountains become simple, stacked forms. His early work of the 1860s is dark and thickly painted; after his years with Pissarro his colours became much brighter.

The collection follows those subjects closely. There are around twenty views of Mont Sainte-Victoire, scenes at Château Noir, the Jas de Bouffan and L'Estaque, and northern landscapes from Pontoise, Auvers and the banks of the Seine and Marne. Still lifes of apples, pears, jugs and ginger jars sit alongside portraits of Madame Cézanne, the artist's son and friends, bathers, card players, and many drawn studies after sculptures and paintings by Rubens and other old masters.

DECORATING WITH CÉZANNE PRINTS
========================================
Cézanne's Provence landscapes, with their earthy ochres, greens and blues, suit a living room or a hallway with good light. A Mont Sainte-Victoire view such as La Montagne Sainte-Victoire vue des Lauves makes a strong single piece above a sofa or sideboard, while several of the smaller Sainte-Victoire or Jas de Bouffan scenes hang well together as a group.

His still lifes are a natural fit for a kitchen or dining room: Still Life with Apples and Peaches or Pommes Et Poires bring fruit, cloth and pottery to the table. For a study or library, try the portraits and card players, or the pencil studies, which look calm and considered in simple frames. The bathers suit a bedroom or a bathroom.

FAQ
========================================
Q: What art movement was Paul Cézanne part of?
A: Cézanne is classed as a Post-Impressionist. He exhibited with the Impressionists in 1874 and 1877, then developed a more structured style that influenced Cubism and much of 20th-century art.

Q: What is Paul Cézanne known for?
A: He is best known for his paintings of Mont Sainte-Victoire, his still lifes of apples, The Card Players and his large bather compositions. His way of building forms from planes of colour made him a key figure for modern painters.

Q: Where can I see Paul Cézanne's original works?
A: Major collections include the Musée d'Orsay and the Musée de l'Orangerie in Paris, the Musée Granet in Aix-en-Provence, the National Gallery in London, the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York and the Philadelphia Museum of Art. His studio at Les Lauves in Aix is open to the public.

Q: How can I recognise Cézanne's style?
A: Look for repeated, often diagonal brushstrokes laid down in patches of colour, simplified shapes, and objects that feel solid and are sometimes slightly tilted. He spoke of treating nature "in terms of the cylinder, the sphere and the cone".

Q: Why did Cézanne paint Mont Sainte-Victoire so many times?
A: The mountain rises over the countryside around Aix, his home town, and he returned to it again and again in his later life, in dozens of oils and watercolours. In an 1878 letter to Zola he described it simply as "a beautiful motif".
