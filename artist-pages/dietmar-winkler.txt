========================================
ABOUT DIETMAR WINKLER
========================================
Dietmar R. Winkler was born in 1938 and trained as a designer in Hamburg, Germany. He later built his career in the United States, both as a working designer and as a teacher.

In 1965 he joined the Office of Publications at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT), the in-house office that designed the Institute's posters and printed matter. Growing demand meant the office needed a third full-time designer, and Winkler joined Jacqueline Casey and Ralph Coburn, who were about a decade older. He brought what Eye magazine calls sophisticated 'European' design to MIT clients, and shared his knowledge of typography, print production and offset printing. He introduced Casey to the split-fountain technique, which blends two inks across a single print run.

Winkler, Casey, Coburn and Muriel Cooper are credited with bringing modern Swiss-style typography to MIT. His posters advertised concerts, lectures, graduate programmes and student groups. One, made for the MIT Black Students Union around 1970, was shown in the Swiss Style: International Graphic Design exhibition at the Museum für Gestaltung in Zurich.

He went on to work as type and design director for Brandeis University, Harvard Business School, MIT and the WGBH Educational Foundation. He became a professor at the University of Massachusetts Dartmouth, where he is now professor emeritus, and has written on design and design education. He sits on the editorial board of advisors of the journal Visible Language. The Library of Congress holds a set of his signed MIT posters.

========================================
STYLE AND SUBJECTS
========================================
Winkler's posters come from the MIT office that developed what became known as the MIT Style: grids, sans serif type and abstract images, made to stand out on busy noticeboards. In his hands the words often become the picture. "Stop" and "Join" are woven together in a circle, course titles cross each other in blue and white letters, and a seminar title repeats down the sheet in black and red. Where he uses images, they are spare: an abstract sun, a winding symbol, a hand on piano keys, shadowy figures in song.

The collection holds 21 of these posters, most of them dated between 1967 and 1970. They advertise MIT graduate study in biology, electrical engineering, ocean engineering, geology and energy, and a joint programme with the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution. There are concert posters for the MIT Chapel, including the Noonhour series and performances by E. Power Biggs and Catharine Crozier, and posters for lecture series, a summer COBOL programming course, an opera and the Wellesley-MIT exchange.

DECORATING WITH WINKLER PRINTS
========================================
Winkler's graduate programme and computing posters suit a home office or study, where their crisp type and clear layouts feel focused rather than busy. COBOL and Massachusetts Institute of Technology graduate study in Electrical Engineering make a good pair, and several of the posters are tall and narrow, which helps in a hallway or between windows.

For a music room, living room or record corner, choose the concert posters: MIT Chapel, with its hand on the piano keys, or E. Power Biggs and the Catherine Crosier organ concert. The type-led pieces, such as Stop – Join, work well in a mid-century modern interior, and a group of three or four in matching frames reads as a small gallery of 1960s design.

FAQ
========================================
Q: What design movement is Dietmar Winkler associated with?
A: He is associated with Swiss-style typography, also called the International Typographic Style. With Muriel Cooper, Jacqueline Casey and Ralph Coburn, he is credited with bringing it to MIT, and one of his posters was shown in the Swiss Style exhibition at the Museum für Gestaltung in Zurich.

Q: What is Dietmar Winkler known for?
A: He is best known for the posters and publications he designed for MIT's Office of Publications from 1965. He is also known as a design educator and writer, and is professor emeritus at the University of Massachusetts Dartmouth.

Q: Where can I see Dietmar Winkler's original posters?
A: The Library of Congress in Washington, D.C. holds a set of his signed MIT posters, including COBOL and MIT Chapel, in its Prints and Photographs collection. The AIGA Design Archives also record MIT brochures he designed in the late 1960s.

Q: How can I recognise a Dietmar Winkler poster?
A: Look for type used as the main image, with words merged, crossed or repeated across the sheet, in a few strong colours. His MIT posters are signed "Dietmar R. Winkler".

Q: Did Dietmar Winkler work with Jacqueline Casey?
A: Yes. He joined Casey and Ralph Coburn in MIT's design office in 1965, and he introduced her to the split-fountain printing effects she later used in her own posters. At least one poster for the MIT Black Students Union is credited to them both.
