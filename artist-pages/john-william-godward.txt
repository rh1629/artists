========================================
ABOUT JOHN WILLIAM GODWARD
========================================
John William Godward was born in Wimbledon, south of London, in 1861, the eldest of five children. His father was an investment clerk at the Law Life Assurance Society. Little is known for certain about his training. He probably took part-time classes, and much of his skill may have been self-taught.

He first exhibited at the Royal Academy in 1887 and soon made his name with paintings of young women in classical settings. As his success grew he left his parents' home for a studio in Chelsea, which he filled with antiques and marble statues. He is often called a protégé of Sir Lawrence Alma-Tadema, and his marble-filled scenes of ancient life are closest in style to Alma-Tadema's.

In 1912 he moved to Italy with one of his models and settled in Rome, near the Villa Borghese. His family disapproved, broke off contact and even cut his image out of family photographs. By then his kind of painting was falling out of favour with the rise of modern art.

Godward returned to England in 1921, in poor health and suffering from depression, and painted far less. He took his own life in Fulham, London, in December 1922, and was buried in Brompton Cemetery. His family burned his papers, and only one photograph of him is known to survive. Today his work is held by the J. Paul Getty Museum in Los Angeles, the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York and the British Museum in London.

========================================
STYLE AND SUBJECTS
========================================
Godward painted women in classical dress, posed on marble terraces, benches and balconies. He researched ancient architecture and dress with care, and he is known for the way he painted contrasting textures: skin, polished marble, animal furs and fine, almost sheer fabrics. Wildflowers and blossoms add touches of colour. His pictures tell almost no story. They are about calm, beauty and the pleasure of looking.

The collection shows this world in depth. There are reclining figures on tiger and leopard skins, as in Mischief and Repose and The Tigerskin; women gazing out to sea from marble balconies, as in The Belvedere and On the Balcony; and quieter head studies such as Far Away Thoughts. Titles like A Pompeian Lady, In the Grove of the Temple of Isis and Outside the Gate of Pompeii set the scene in the ancient world, and the dated works run from Dora (1887) to The Day Dream (1920).

DECORATING WITH GODWARD PRINTS
========================================
Godward's warm palette of ochre, rose, violet and sea blue suits a calm living room or bedroom. The Belvedere, with its deep red drapery against a bright sea, makes a strong focal point above a sofa or mantelpiece, while The Day Dream, a sleeping woman on a green couch, is a natural choice above a bed.

Tall, narrow pictures like On the Balcony and Waiting for the Procession fit slim spaces such as a hallway or the wall beside a staircase. The round Violets, Sweet Violets, in soft pinks and lilacs, suits a dressing room, or a bathroom with marble or pale tiles that echo the marble in the painting.

FAQ
========================================
Q: Was John William Godward a Pre-Raphaelite?
A: No. He is often mistaken for one, but he was a Victorian neo-classicist, working in the classical tradition of Frederic Leighton and, above all, Lawrence Alma-Tadema.

Q: What is John William Godward known for?
A: He is known for paintings of young women in classical settings, and for his skill with contrasting textures: skin, marble, fur and fabric. Dolce Far Niente and Mischief and Repose are among his best-known pictures.

Q: Where can I see John William Godward's original paintings?
A: The J. Paul Getty Museum in Los Angeles holds Mischief and Repose (1895), and his work is also in the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York and the British Museum in London. Many of his paintings are in private collections.

Q: How can I recognise Godward's style?
A: Look for one or two women in softly coloured classical robes, set against polished, veined marble, often with an animal skin or flowers close by. The mood is still and dreamy, with little or no story.

Q: Was Godward a pupil of Alma-Tadema?
A: He is often described as Alma-Tadema's protégé, and his style is closest to Alma-Tadema's, but it is not known how much contact the two men actually had.
