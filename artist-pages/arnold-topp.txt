ABOUT ARNOLD TOPP
========================================
Arnold Topp was born in Soest, Westphalia, on 8 March 1887, the son of a locomotive driver. He trained as a teacher in Soest, finishing in 1908, and then taught at a village school in Erndtebrück. From 1904 he was a friend of the younger Soest painter Wilhelm Morgner, whose work made a strong impression on him. From 1909 to 1911 he studied at the drawing-teacher seminar of the Düsseldorf School of Applied Arts under Lothar von Kunowski, where he first met the Rhenish Expressionists.

In 1913 Topp took a teaching post at the Saldernsches Realgymnasium in Brandenburg an der Havel, and in 1914 he married the painter Else Hollerbaum. He was called up in 1915 and wounded at Verdun, and returned to teaching in 1918. Through the dealer Alfred Flechtheim he had made contact with Herwarth Walden, and from 1915 until about 1926 he showed regularly at Walden's Der Sturm gallery in Berlin. His first solo exhibition there came in 1917, and he also showed with artists such as Kurt Schwitters.

In Berlin he befriended the architect Bruno Taut and the painter Georg Muche, and in 1918 he was a founding member of the Arbeitsrat für Kunst (Workers' Council for Art). Until 1929 his work was exhibited in Germany, the USA, the Soviet Union, Japan and France. He became a senior teacher in 1927 and joined the National Socialist Teachers' League in 1933. In 1937 four of his works were confiscated from public collections as "degenerate", and two paintings were shown in the 1938 Degenerate Art exhibitions in Düsseldorf and Hamburg.

In 1940 Topp was transferred to Meseritz, now Międzyrzecz in Poland. He was called up in 1945 and went missing in combat that year, probably taken prisoner by the Soviet army. His wife fled, leaving their house, studio and his works behind, and much of his output was lost. In 1961 a court in Soest declared him dead, with the date of death set at 31 December 1945. His work is held by the Museum Wilhelm Morgner in Soest, the Neue Nationalgalerie in Berlin, the Staatsgalerie Stuttgart, the National Gallery of Art in Washington, the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York and the British Museum in London.

========================================
STYLE AND SUBJECTS
========================================
Topp is known for colourful Cubist paintings that mix figures, landscape and architecture, held together by a strong sense of movement and glowing colour. Houses, churches, trees and moons break into sharp, overlapping planes. A 1918 series of cathedral pictures turned medieval buildings into Cubist, constructed forms, and writers have described his "cosmic visions", in which figures and lines of script meet abstraction. From 1916 he also made reverse glass paintings, and by 1923 he was making purely abstract work. When abstract art fell out of fashion around 1923–24, he stayed loyal to Walden and Der Sturm.

The collection shows this range. Weltenkreisen (1919) and Zwei Welten (Two Worlds) are bright, faceted pictures in red, yellow and blue, while in Gelbroter Mond über stürzenden Häusern a whole village seems to tumble beneath the moon. Schlafende Gestalten in nächtlicher Gebirgslandschaft turns to deep blues and whites under a starry sky, and Höfe der Mietskasernen moves to the city in greys, black and cream. Malven (1923) and the two untitled works lean towards pure abstraction.

DECORATING WITH TOPP PRINTS
========================================
Topp's brightest pictures, such as Zwei Welten (Two Worlds) and Gelbroter Mond über stürzenden Häusern, are full of strong reds, yellows and blues. They make a confident focal point above a sofa or at the end of a hallway, and they sit well against plain white or dark walls with simple modern furniture. Their faceted shapes also suit a home office or a creative workspace.

For a calmer room, Schlafende Gestalten in nächtlicher Gebirgslandschaft, with its blues, whites and starry sky, is a good choice for a bedroom. Höfe der Mietskasernen, in greys, black and cream, works in a study or a pared-back living room and looks sharp in a black frame. Malven, with its deep blues, greens and oranges, holds its own in a dining room.
