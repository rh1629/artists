========================================
ABOUT OHARA KOSON
========================================
Ohara Koson was born Ohara Matao in Kanazawa in 1877 and died in Tokyo in 1945. He studied painting in the Nihonga tradition with Suzuki Kason, and took the second character of his art name, Koson, from his teacher's name.

He moved to Tokyo in the late 1890s, and around 1900 he taught at the Tokyo School of Fine Arts. There he met Ernest Fenollosa, the American scholar of Japanese art, who encouraged him to design woodblock prints and helped him find buyers in the United States. In the same years Koson showed paintings at public exhibitions and won several prizes.

His early prints included triptychs of the Russo-Japanese War of 1904–05, but he soon settled on kachō-e, bird-and-flower prints. From around 1906 he designed them for the publishers Kokkeidō and Daikokuya, signing them Koson, mostly in tall, narrow formats. After the 1923 Great Kantō earthquake destroyed many printing blocks, the publisher Watanabe Shōzaburō took him on in 1926. These later prints, signed Shōson, were larger, brighter in colour and made mainly for buyers abroad. In the early 1930s he also worked for the publisher Kawaguchi under the name Hōson.

Koson designed around 500 prints and was still at work until at least 1935. Because so many were sold overseas, he was little known in Japan during his lifetime, and Japanese interest grew only from the 1970s. The Rijksmuseum in Amsterdam, which holds a large group of his prints, gave him an exhibition in 2001. His work is also in the British Museum, the Museum of Fine Arts in Boston, the Brooklyn Museum and the Art Institute of Chicago.

========================================
STYLE AND SUBJECTS
========================================
Koson's prints are simple and calm. Most show a single bird or a pair, perched on a branch or in flight, against a plain or softly shaded ground. He returned again and again to the moon, to falling snow and to rain, which give many scenes a hushed, seasonal mood. His early prints are softer and flatter in colour, while the Shōson prints made for Watanabe use stronger, brighter colour.

Birds make up most of our collection: crows, herons and egrets, geese and ducks, eagles and hawks, kingfishers, owls, pheasants, cockatoos and peacocks. Flowers play a large part too, with irises, lotus, wisteria, peonies and plum and cherry blossom. There are also carp, monkeys, deer, puppies and a frog with tadpoles, plus a small group of figure scenes such as Two women in the rain and Torii in the snow. The dated works run from about 1900 to 1936.

========================================
DECORATING WITH KOSON PRINTS
========================================
Koson's soft greys, creams and pale blues suit calm rooms: a bedroom, a reading corner or a hallway where you want something restful. Most of his prints are tall and narrow, so they fit the slim wall beside a door or window, or hang well as a vertical pair. Plum blossom and full moon and Kingfisher with Lotus Flower are quiet, pale choices that work well together.

For more colour, Swallows and Wisteria brings purple-blue blossom to a living room or garden room, and Geese flying over reed has a soft aqua sky. Wider prints such as Mallards and lotus under snow sit well above a sideboard or desk, and the carp and lotus prints are a natural fit for a bathroom.

FAQ
========================================
Q: What art movement was Ohara Koson part of?
A: Koson is linked to shin-hanga, the "new prints" movement in 20th-century Japanese woodblock printing, and especially to its leading publisher, Watanabe Shōzaburō. He trained as a painter in the Nihonga tradition.

Q: What is Ohara Koson known for?
A: He is best known for kachō-e, bird-and-flower prints, of which he designed around 500. Crows, herons, geese and songbirds, often under the moon or in snow and rain, are among his best-loved subjects.

Q: Where can I see Ohara Koson's original works?
A: The Rijksmuseum in Amsterdam holds a large group of his prints. His work is also in the British Museum, the Museum of Fine Arts in Boston, the Brooklyn Museum and the Art Institute of Chicago.

Q: What makes Ohara Koson's style recognizable?
A: Look for a single bird or a pair on a branch or in flight, set against a plain background, often with a full moon, falling snow or rain. Many of his prints are in a tall, narrow format.

Q: Are Koson, Shōson and Hōson the same artist?
A: Yes. He signed his prints Koson for his early publishers, Shōson for Watanabe Shōzaburō from 1926, and Hōson for the publisher Kawaguchi in the early 1930s. Some paintings signed Shōson may be by a different artist, Hirooka Shōson.
