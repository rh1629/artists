========================================
ABOUT OHARA KOSON
========================================
Ohara Koson was born Ohara Matao in Kanazawa in 1877 and died in Tokyo in 1945. He studied painting with Suzuki Kason, and when his training was finished he took the name Koson, said to mean "son of Kason", in tribute to his teacher.

After moving to Tokyo in the late 1890s, he showed paintings at public exhibitions between 1899 and 1903 and won several prizes. The American scholar Ernest Fenollosa encouraged him to paint birds and flowers for Western buyers, and an exhibition in New York in 1899 included his work. He began designing woodblock prints around 1901. During the Russo-Japanese War of 1904–05 he designed triptychs of battle scenes, but birds and flowers soon became his main subject.

In the late Meiji period he worked with the publishers Daikokuya (Matsuki Heikichi) and Kokkeidō (Akiyama Buemon), producing hundreds of bird-and-flower prints, many in small square or narrow upright formats. From about 1926 he worked with Watanabe Shōzaburō, the publisher who led the shin-hanga movement, and signed those prints Shōson. Later prints signed Hōson were issued by the publishers Kawaguchi and Sakai. He was still designing prints until at least 1935, and made around 500 in all.

His prints sold well abroad, especially in the United States, while he stayed little known in Japan for much of his life. Today his work is held by the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, the British Museum, the Rijksmuseum, the Brooklyn Museum and the National Museum of Modern Art, Tokyo, among others. Exhibitions in Japan in 1998, 2018 and 2019 brought him wider attention at home, and the Manggha Museum in Kraków held a large retrospective in 2021.

========================================
STYLE AND SUBJECTS
========================================
Koson worked in kachō-e, the Japanese tradition of bird-and-flower pictures. His designs usually pair one or two birds or animals with a single plant, season or kind of weather: a crow on a snowy branch, an egret in the rain, swallows with wisteria, a heron under the moon. For Kokkeidō he made waterside scenes using silhouettes, and the prints he made for Watanabe from the mid-1920s are known for their unusually vivid colour. Shin-hanga itself was a revival of the old ukiyo-e print that borrowed Western ideas of light and mood while keeping traditional Japanese subjects.

The collection shows that range well. Birds lead the way, from kingfishers, owls and cuckoos to hawks, eagles, geese and mandarin ducks, set among lotus, irises, peonies, and plum and cherry blossom. There are also monkeys, deer, carp and puppies, many scenes lit by the moon or covered in snow, and a handful of figures and townscapes, such as Yanagibashi no yuki (Snow on the Willow Bridge) from 1927 and Umbrellas in Snow.

========================================
DECORATING WITH KOSON PRINTS
========================================
Koson's birds and flowers are calm and uncluttered, so they suit rooms where you want quiet: a bedroom, a hallway or a reading corner. Water birds and lotus scenes, such as Kingfisher with Lotus Flower or Herons in shallow water, work well in a bathroom or a restful living room, and two or three hung together make a gentle group.

For a seasonal touch, snow scenes such as Torii in the snow bring a cool, quiet mood to winter walls, while blossom prints like Swallows and Wisteria add spring colour to a kitchen or dining room. His monkeys, puppies and chicks are a soft choice for a child's room, and a row of moonlit prints suits a dark accent wall in a living room.

FAQ
========================================
Q: What art movement was Ohara Koson part of?
A: Koson is linked with shin-hanga ("new prints"), an early 20th-century revival of Japanese woodblock printing, and with the souvenir prints called shinsaku-hanga that came before it. His subject was kachō-e, the bird-and-flower tradition.

Q: What is Ohara Koson known for?
A: He is best known for bird-and-flower woodblock prints, of which he designed around 500. The prints he made for the publisher Watanabe Shōzaburō are noted for their unusually vivid colour.

Q: Where can I see Ohara Koson's original works?
A: His prints are held by the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, the British Museum, the Rijksmuseum in Amsterdam, the Brooklyn Museum and the National Museum of Modern Art, Tokyo. The Arthur M. Sackler Gallery also has a large group, left to it by the dealer Robert Muller.

Q: How can I recognise Ohara Koson's style?
A: Look for one or two birds or animals paired with a single plant and a season or weather: snow, rain, blossom or the moon. Depending on the date and publisher, the signature reads Koson, Shōson or Hōson.

Q: Are Ohara Shōson and Ohara Hōson different artists?
A: No. They are the same artist, Ohara Koson, using different signatures. He signed Koson on his early prints, Shōson on the prints published by Watanabe from about 1926, and Hōson on prints issued by Kawaguchi and Sakai.
