ABOUT PASCAL-ADOLPHE-JEAN DAGNAN-BOUVERET
========================================
Pascal-Adolphe-Jean Dagnan-Bouveret was born in Paris on 7 January 1852, the son of a tailor. After his father emigrated to Brazil, he was brought up by his grandfather, Gabriel Bouveret, in Melun, and he later added his grandfather's name to his own. In 1869 he entered the École des Beaux-Arts in Paris, studying under Alexandre Cabanel and then Jean-Léon Gérôme. There he became friends with Jules Bastien-Lepage, the Finnish painter Albert Edelfelt and Gustave Courtois.

He first showed at the Salon in the mid-1870s and came second in the Prix de Rome in 1876. He then went to Franche-Comté in eastern France and turned to scenes of everyday life. In 1880 his painting An Accident won a first-class medal at the Salon. From the 1880s he shared a studio with Courtois in Neuilly-sur-Seine, near Paris, and he was one of the first painters to use photography to make his pictures more true to life. From 1885 he often visited Brittany, which inspired many of his best-known paintings, and in 1888 he travelled to Tétouan with the painters Louis-Auguste Girardot and Jules-Alexis Muenier.

By now he was seen as a leading modern artist. He won a medal of honour at the Salon, and in the early 1890s he was made an Officer of the Legion of Honour. In the later 1890s he turned to religious subjects and moved in Symbolist circles, and his large Last Supper was shown at the Salon du Champ-de-Mars in 1896. In 1900 he received a grand prize at the Exposition Universelle for his work as a whole and was elected to the Académie des Beaux-Arts, which he went on to lead as president in 1914.

He was much admired in the United States, where the Frick family gave him commissions, and at the height of his career he painted mainly portraits. He married Anne-Marie Walter, a cousin of Gustave Courtois. Dagnan-Bouveret died at his home in Quincey, in the Haute-Saône, on 3 July 1929, and is buried in the Passy Cemetery in Paris. His work is held by the Musée d'Orsay, the Metropolitan Museum of Art, the Art Institute of Chicago, the Ateneum in Helsinki and the Pushkin Museum in Moscow.

STYLE AND SUBJECTS
========================================
Dagnan-Bouveret painted with the smooth, careful finish of the academic studios, but he turned it on ordinary people: village families, peasants and the women of Brittany in their black dresses and white caps. He often worked from photographs, which gives his scenes their sharp detail and natural poses. Faces are painted with great tenderness, and soft light falls on white cloth, lace and skin. From the later 1890s his religious paintings took on a dreamier mood, with glowing light and figures that seem to step out of shadow.

The collection follows this whole path. Early scenes such as Wedding in the Photographer's Studio, An Accident and Blessing of the Young Couple Before Marriage show village and family life. The Brittany works include The Pardon in Brittany, Breton Women at a Pardon and a study for it, and the portrait Woman from Brittany. Religious works run from Madonna of the Rose to Consolatrix Afflictorum and Christ and the Pilgrims at Emmaus. There are also portraits of his friends Gustave Courtois and Jules-Alexis Muenier, a late river landscape, Willows by a Stream, and many studies of hands, arms and figures.

FAQ
========================================
Q: What art movement was Pascal-Adolphe-Jean Dagnan-Bouveret part of?
A: He was one of the leading members of the Naturalist school in France, painting everyday life with close, realistic detail. He trained in the academic tradition under Cabanel and Gérôme, and in the later 1890s he moved in Symbolist circles.

Q: What is Dagnan-Bouveret known for?
A: He is best known for his scenes of peasant and village life, especially his paintings of Brittany such as The Pardon in Brittany and Breton Women at a Pardon. He is also known for his religious paintings and, later in his career, his portraits.

Q: Where can I see Dagnan-Bouveret's original works?
A: Public collections include the Musée d'Orsay in Paris, the Musée des Beaux-Arts in Lyon, the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York, the Art Institute of Chicago and the National Gallery of Art in Washington. His work is also in the Ateneum in Helsinki, the Pushkin Museum in Moscow and the Musée Jean-Léon Gérôme in Vesoul, to which he gave many works.

Q: Did Dagnan-Bouveret paint from photographs?
A: Yes. He was one of the first painters to use the new medium of photography to bring greater realism to his paintings. The Musée d'Orsay holds more than sixty photographs of his family and studio.

Q: How can I recognise a Dagnan-Bouveret painting?
A: Look for ordinary people painted with a smooth, highly finished surface and lifelike poses, often in soft, even light. Breton women in black dresses and white caps are his best-known subject.
