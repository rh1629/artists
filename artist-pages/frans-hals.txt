========================================
ABOUT FRANS HALS
========================================
Frans Hals was born in Antwerp in 1582 or 1583, the son of the cloth merchant Franchois Fransz Hals and his second wife, Adriaentje van Geertenryck. After the Fall of Antwerp (1584–1585) the family moved north to Haarlem in the new Dutch Republic, where they are first recorded in 1591. Hals lived in Haarlem for the rest of his life. He is said to have trained with the Flemish painter and writer Karel van Mander, though little of Van Mander's influence shows in his work.

In 1610 he joined the Haarlem Guild of Saint Luke and also worked as a restorer of paintings for the town council. His earliest known dated work is from 1611, and his breakthrough came with a life-size group portrait, The Banquet of the Officers of the St George Militia Company in 1616. He served in Haarlem's civic guard himself, and he painted the officers of the St George company three times. Around 1610 he married Anneke Harmensdr, who died in 1615. In 1617 he married Lysbeth Reyniers, and at least five of his sons became painters.

Unlike Rembrandt and other painters who moved to follow their patrons, Hals stayed in Haarlem and asked his sitters to come to him. When he took on a militia portrait in Amsterdam, he refused to paint there, and the work, now known as The Meagre Company, was finished by Pieter Codde. Through the 1620s and 1630s he painted Haarlem's wealthy citizens, many pairs of wedding portraits, and the officers of its civic guards and the governors of its hospitals. In 1644 he became chairman of the Guild of Saint Luke, and in 1649 he painted the philosopher René Descartes.

Hals's work was in demand for most of his life, but in old age his style fell out of fashion and he struggled with debt. In 1652 his belongings were sold to pay a baker, and in 1664 the city granted him a yearly pension of 200 guilders. Around the same time he painted his sober group portraits of the regents and regentesses of Haarlem's Old Men's Almshouse. He died in Haarlem on 26 August 1666 and was buried in the Grote Kerk. His reputation faded for two centuries, until the critic Théophile Thoré-Bürger and the Impressionists revived interest in him from the 1860s. Several of his most important works are now in the Frans Hals Museum in Haarlem, and others hang in the Rijksmuseum, the Mauritshuis, the Louvre and the Wallace Collection.

========================================
STYLE AND SUBJECTS
========================================
Hals painted with loose, visible brushstrokes at a time when most Dutch painters gave their work a smooth finish. A few quick, fluid strokes could set down a lace collar, a hand or a smile, and his sitters often look as if they are about to speak. He favoured clear daylight and silvery tones rather than Rembrandt's golden gloom. His early portraits are full of colour, while in later ones black takes over, reflecting the sober dress of his Protestant sitters, and his brushwork grows ever freer. A favourite pose has the sitter leaning casually over the back of a chair. Two centuries after his death, painters such as Manet, Monet and Van Gogh admired and learned from his free handling of paint.

Hals was a portrait specialist, and our collection reflects that. Most of its prints are portraits of Haarlem's merchants, officials and their wives, including Isaac Massa, Aletta Hanemans, Feyntje van Steenkiste and Willem Coymans, along with a militia group portrait. He also painted lively character pieces for the open market, and these make up the most cheerful part of the collection: laughing children, fisherboys by the shore, young musicians with lutes and flutes, a rommelpot player surrounded by children, and the Haarlem figure Malle Babbe. There is also a rare religious subject, Saint John the Evangelist.

========================================
DECORATING WITH HALS PRINTS
========================================
Hals's formal portraits, with their deep blacks, crisp white ruffs and warm skin tones, suit a study, library or hallway. Many were painted as pairs, with the husband on the left and the wife on the right, and they still work well hung that way. Portrait of an Elderly Man, traditionally called Heer Bodolphe and Portrait of an Elderly Woman, traditionally called Mevrouw Bodolphe make a natural pair above a sideboard or either side of a doorway. A wide group such as The Meagre Company makes a strong focal point above a sofa or fireplace.

For warmth and humour, turn to the character pieces. The Merry Drinker, in his ochre jacket, and the crowded, colourful Merrymakers at Shrovetide bring a convivial mood to a dining room, kitchen or home bar. Smiling children such as A Fisherboy suit a family room, landing or child's bedroom, and Boy with a Lute would sit well in a music room or near a piano.

FAQ
========================================
Q: What art movement was Frans Hals part of?
A: Hals was a Dutch Golden Age painter who worked in Haarlem in the 17th century. He is often ranked with Rembrandt and Vermeer as one of the three most important Dutch painters of that era.

Q: What is Frans Hals known for?
A: He is known for lively portraits painted with loose, visible brushstrokes, and for capturing passing expressions, from the half smile of The Laughing Cavalier to the grin of Malle Babbe. He also painted large group portraits of Haarlem's civic guards and of the governors of its hospitals and almshouses.

Q: Where can I see Frans Hals's original paintings?
A: The Frans Hals Museum in Haarlem holds several of his most important works, including his militia and regents group portraits. Others are in the Rijksmuseum in Amsterdam, the Mauritshuis in The Hague, the Wallace Collection and the National Gallery in London, the Louvre in Paris and the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York.

Q: What makes Frans Hals's style recognisable?
A: Look for quick, confident brushstrokes that stay visible on the surface, and for faces that seem caught mid-laugh or mid-word. His sitters often wear black with white ruffs or lace collars, and many lean casually on a chair or turn towards the viewer.

Q: Was Frans Hals really a heavy drinker?
A: There is no direct evidence that he was. The stories come largely from embellished anecdotes by early biographers such as Arnold Houbraken, and older claims that he mistreated his first wife turned out to concern another Haarlem man of the same name.
