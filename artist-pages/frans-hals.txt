========================================
ABOUT FRANS HALS
========================================
Frans Hals was born in Antwerp in 1582 or 1583. His father came from Mechelen and worked in the cloth trade. Like many families from the southern Netherlands, they left at the time of the Spanish siege of Antwerp in 1584–85, and by 1591 they were living in Haarlem. Hals spent the rest of his life there. He is said to have trained with the Flemish painter and writer Karel van Mander, although little of van Mander's influence shows in his work.

In 1610 he joined Haarlem's Guild of Saint Luke, and he also worked as a restorer of the town's paintings. The city council had taken Catholic religious art out of the churches, and with the market for religious pictures gone, Hals built his career on portraits. His earliest known dated work is from 1611. In 1616 he painted his first life-size group portrait of the officers of Haarlem's St George civic guard, and that year he visited Antwerp to study the work of Rubens and Van Dyck. He served in the civic guard himself from 1612 to 1624, and he painted its officers several more times, up to 1639.

Around 1610 he married Anneke Harmensdochter, who died in 1615. In 1617 he married Lysbeth Reyniers, and they had a large family. His younger brother Dirck Hals was also a painter, as were several of his sons. Unlike many painters of his day, Hals did not travel to his clients but asked them to come to Haarlem. When he refused to paint in Amsterdam, a militia portrait he had begun there, The Meagre Company, was finished by Pieter Codde. In 1644 he became chairman of the Haarlem Guild of Saint Luke. Later stories that he drank heavily come from an early 18th-century biographer, and there is no evidence for them.

His work was in demand for much of his life, but in old age he fell out of fashion and into debt. In 1652 his belongings were sold to settle a debt with a baker, and from 1664 the city of Haarlem paid him a yearly pension. In those last years he painted the regents and regentesses of the Old Men's Almshouse. He died in Haarlem on 26 August 1666 and was buried in the Grote Kerk. His reputation faded for two centuries, until critics and painters rediscovered him from the 1860s. Several of his most important group portraits now hang in the Frans Hals Museum in Haarlem, and his work is held by museums across Europe and North America.

========================================
STYLE AND SUBJECTS
========================================
Hals left his brushstrokes visible at a time when most painters smoothed them away. Seen close up, a lace collar, a hand or a smile is a few quick, fluid strokes; from a distance it becomes a living face. Studies of his technique show that he usually built his pictures up in layers rather than painting them in one go. He favoured daylight and a silvery sheen, and his sitters often have a glint in the eye or the beginning of a smile. Over time his palette grew more sober, and in his later portraits black dominates, matching the dark clothes of his clients. In the 19th century this free handling was admired by painters such as Manet, Monet and Van Gogh.

Hals was a portrait specialist and is not known to have painted landscapes or still lifes, and most of our collection reflects that. There are nearly 50 portraits of men and women, from a Haarlem alderman to an elderly couple painted as a matching pair, Heer and Mevrouw Bodolphe. Alongside them are his livelier scenes of everyday life: fisher boys on the dunes, boys with a lute and a flute, the Rommel-Pot Player surrounded by children, the Merry Drinker with his glass, and Malle Babbe with her owl and tankard. The collection also includes The Meagre Company and his only known portrait of a married couple on a single canvas.

========================================
DECORATING WITH HALS PRINTS
========================================
Most of Hals's portraits are dark, with deep blacks and browns lit by crisp white ruffs and collars. They suit a study, library or hallway, and stand out well against pale walls in simple black or gilt frames. Pairs work especially well: hang Portrait of an Elderly Man, traditionally called Heer Bodolphe beside its companion, Portrait of an Elderly Woman, traditionally called Mevrouw Bodolphe, or group several portraits together on a staircase wall.

For more warmth and humour, turn to his scenes of everyday life. The Merry Drinker and Young Man and Woman in an Inn suit a dining room, kitchen or home bar, while Boy with a Lute or Singing Boy with Flute brings music into a living room. Fisherboy, with its open sky and dunes, adds light to a hallway or a coastal-style room, and the crowded, colourful Merrymakers at Shrovetide makes a lively focal point above a sideboard.

FAQ
========================================
Q: What art movement was Frans Hals part of?
A: Hals was a painter of the Dutch Golden Age, and his work belongs to the Baroque. He is often ranked with Rembrandt and Vermeer as one of the three leading Dutch painters of the 17th century.

Q: What is Frans Hals known for?
A: He is best known for lively portraits painted with loose, visible brushstrokes, such as The Laughing Cavalier, and for large group portraits of Haarlem's civic guard officers and almshouse regents. He also painted laughing children, musicians and drinkers.

Q: Where can I see Frans Hals's original works?
A: The Frans Hals Museum in Haarlem holds his civic guard and regents group portraits. The Rijksmuseum in Amsterdam has The Merry Drinker, The Meagre Company and the Massa marriage portrait, and The Laughing Cavalier is in the Wallace Collection in London. The Mauritshuis in The Hague, the Louvre, the Gemäldegalerie in Berlin and the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York also hold his work.

Q: How can I recognise Frans Hals's style?
A: Look for quick, visible brushstrokes, especially in lace collars, hands and hair, and for faces caught mid-expression, often smiling. His earlier works are more colourful, while his later portraits are dominated by black and white.

Q: Is Frans Hals the same artist as Frans Hals the Younger?
A: No. Frans Hals the Younger (1618–1669) was his son, one of five sons who became painters. That is why the father is sometimes called Frans Hals the Elder.
