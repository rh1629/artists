ABOUT LOUIS SOUTTER
========================================
Louis Soutter was born in Morges, Switzerland, in 1871. His father was a pharmacist and his mother taught singing, and through her he was a cousin of the architect Le Corbusier. He studied engineering in Lausanne and architecture in Geneva, then in 1892 went to Brussels to study the violin with Eugène Ysaÿe at the Royal Conservatory. In 1895 he turned to painting, first in Lausanne and Geneva, then at the Académie Colarossi in Paris under Jean-Paul Laurens and Jean-Joseph Benjamin-Constant.

In 1897 he moved to the United States, and that summer he married Madge Fursman, an American he had met as a fellow student in Brussels, in Colorado Springs. By 1898 he was head of the art department at Colorado College. The marriage ended in divorce in 1903 and he returned to Europe. Back in Switzerland he played the violin in orchestras in Geneva and Lausanne, and later in small ensembles at hotels and resorts.

In 1923 he was placed in a home for elderly people at Ballaigues, in the Vaud Jura, where he lived for the rest of his life. It was a hospice, not the psychiatric hospital it is sometimes said to be. There he filled school notebooks with pen and pencil drawings. Around 1927 Le Corbusier found him there, bought his drawings and gave him better paper and ink. In the late 1930s arthritis stiffened his hands, and he began to paint with his fingers, dipping them straight into ink or paint.

Le Corbusier wrote about him in the journal Minotaure in 1936 and helped arrange exhibitions in Hartford in 1936, Lausanne in 1937 and New York in 1939. Soutter died at Ballaigues in 1942. In 1945 Jean Dubuffet discovered his work and linked it with Art Brut. Today his work is held by the Musée cantonal des Beaux-Arts in Lausanne, the Kunstmuseum Basel, the Kunsthaus Zürich, the Centre Pompidou in Paris and the Museum of Modern Art in New York.

========================================
STYLE AND SUBJECTS
========================================
Soutter's work changed sharply during his years at Ballaigues. The notebook drawings of the 1920s are dense with fine pen and pencil lines, and in the 1930s he moved on to larger ink drawings on better paper. The late finger paintings are different again: simple, silhouette-like figures in black India ink, sometimes with gouache, pressed straight onto the paper by hand. The Passion of Christ, and above all the Crucifixion, comes back again and again, along with death and the question of what follows it.

The collection shows both sides. Les P, Fries, L'Auréole and Moving Girls are crowded, hatched line drawings of figures. Most of the rest are bold black figure paintings, such as Le baiser de Judas, The Pharaoh's Virgins and Rubing Day, where dancing or running silhouettes fill the sheet. A few add colour: the grinning yellow face of Tête de face, the vivid portrait L'Américaine doit être plus grande, and the red, orange and blue flowers of Le bouquet d'autrefois.

DECORATING WITH SOUTTER PRINTS
========================================
Soutter's black figure paintings are graphic and strong, so give them room. The Pharaoh's Virgins or Hovering work well above a sofa or along a hallway, against a white or warm neutral wall, where the dark shapes read clearly from across the room. Hung in a row, two or three of them make a frieze of movement.

The line drawings, such as Moving Girls, suit a study or reading corner, where they reward a closer look. For colour, Le bouquet d'autrefois brings reds, oranges and blues to a kitchen or dining room, and Tête de face, with its yellow face on black, adds a bold, slightly playful note to a modern living room or workspace.

FAQ
========================================
Q: Was Louis Soutter an Art Brut artist?
A: He is often linked with Art Brut and outsider art. Jean Dubuffet first placed him in that category and later withdrew him, and some critics argue that his training and knowledge of art set him apart from it.

Q: What is Louis Soutter known for?
A: He is best known for the finger paintings of his last years, simple black figures pressed onto paper by hand, and for the thousands of drawings he made at Ballaigues. A catalogue of his work lists about 2,850 drawings.

Q: Where can I see Louis Soutter's original works?
A: His work is held by the Musée cantonal des Beaux-Arts in Lausanne, the Kunstmuseum Basel, the Kunsthaus Zürich, the Aargauer Kunsthaus, the Centre Pompidou in Paris and the Museum of Modern Art in New York.

Q: How can I recognise Louis Soutter's style?
A: In the late work, look for black, silhouette-like figures, often dancing or crowded together, with soft, hand-pressed edges. His earlier drawings are dense webs of fine pen and pencil lines.

Q: Was Louis Soutter related to Le Corbusier?
A: Yes. The architect Le Corbusier was his cousin on his mother's side. He found Soutter at Ballaigues around 1927, supplied him with materials and helped arrange his exhibitions in the 1930s.
