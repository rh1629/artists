========================================
ABOUT FRANCES HODGKINS
========================================
Frances Hodgkins was born in Dunedin, New Zealand, on 28 April 1869. Her father, William Mathew Hodgkins, was a lawyer and a keen amateur watercolourist. She took classes from the visiting Italian painter G. P. Nerli in 1893 and studied at the Dunedin School of Art in 1895–96.

In 1901 she sailed for Europe, where she lived for the rest of her life apart from two return visits. She joined Norman Garstin's summer sketching classes in France in 1901 and 1902, and spent the winter of 1902–03 painting in Morocco. In 1910 she became the first woman to teach at the Académie Colarossi in Paris, and she went on to run her own watercolour school there.

During the First World War she lived in St Ives, Cornwall, where she met the painter Cedric Morris, who became a lifelong friend. In 1925–26 she designed fabrics for the Calico Printers' Association in Manchester. Morris introduced her to the Seven and Five Society, a London group of modern artists, and she exhibited with them from 1929 to 1934. In the 1930s she painted in Ibiza and at Tossa de Mar in Spain, in Somerset and in Wales, and in 1940 she was selected for the British pavilion at the Venice Biennale, although the war stopped the work reaching Venice.

From 1934 she worked in Dorset, settling at Corfe Castle in 1940. She died near Dorchester on 13 May 1947. Her work is held by Auckland Art Gallery Toi o Tāmaki, Te Papa, Dunedin Public Art Gallery, the Hocken Collections, Christchurch Art Gallery, Tate and the National Galleries of Scotland.

========================================
STYLE AND SUBJECTS
========================================
Hodgkins began as a watercolourist, painting people and places on her travels. From the late 1920s her style became fully modern: forms were simplified, and colour and the balance between colours did most of the work. Her best-known theme, drawn from Picasso and Matisse, sets a still life in the foreground against a landscape behind it. Late in life she turned to oil and to gouache, a watercolour thickened with white, which gave her forms more weight.

The collection covers the whole of that journey. Early watercolours show orange sellers in Tangier, Dutch canals and street scenes, and the market place at San Remo. Portraits include Arthur Lett-Haines, Cedric Morris's partner, and the newly married Katharine and Anthony West. Eight textile designs sit beside still lifes of jugs, plates, eggs and melons, and late landscapes take in Ibiza, the Pembrokeshire village of Solva, Bradford on Tone in Somerset and the Purbeck countryside of Dorset.

FAQ
========================================
Q: What art movement was Frances Hodgkins part of?
A: Hodgkins is counted among the leading modernist painters in Britain. She was a member of the Seven and Five Society, a London group of modern artists, from 1929 to 1934.

Q: What is Frances Hodgkins known for?
A: She is best known for her late paintings that set a still life against a landscape, painted with simplified forms and rich colour. She was also the first woman to teach at the Académie Colarossi in Paris, in 1910.

Q: Where can I see Frances Hodgkins's original works?
A: In New Zealand, her work is held by Auckland Art Gallery Toi o Tāmaki, Te Papa, Dunedin Public Art Gallery, the Hocken Collections and Christchurch Art Gallery. In Britain, Tate and the National Galleries of Scotland hold her paintings.

Q: Did Frances Hodgkins design textiles?
A: Yes. In 1925–26 she worked as a fabric designer for the Calico Printers' Association in Manchester, and her employer sent her to the 1925 decorative arts exhibition in Paris.

Q: Why was Frances Hodgkins's Pleasure Garden controversial?
A: When local subscribers offered it to the city's art gallery after her death, Christchurch City Council turned it down as too avant-garde. After a long public campaign, the city accepted it in 1951, and it is now in Christchurch Art Gallery.
