ABOUT JEAN-HONORÉ FRAGONARD
========================================
Jean-Honoré Fragonard was born in Grasse, in the south of France, on 5 April 1732, the only child of a glover. His family moved to Paris when he was six. He studied briefly with Jean-Siméon Chardin and then with François Boucher, who soon trusted him to paint copies of his own pictures. In 1752 he won the Prix de Rome with Jeroboam Sacrificing to Idols, and studied for three more years under Charles-André van Loo.

In 1756 he went to the French Academy in Rome, where he stayed until 1761. He became close to the painter Hubert Robert, and in 1760 the two toured Italy together, sketching gardens full of fountains, grottoes, temples and terraces. In these years the Abbé de Saint-Non became his patron. Fragonard also studied the Dutch and Flemish masters, among them Rubens and Rembrandt, and in Venice the work of Giovanni Battista Tiepolo.

Back in Paris, his Coresus Sacrificing Himself to Save Callirhoe won him entry to the Academy in 1765. The king bought it and had it reproduced at the Gobelins tapestry works. But Fragonard did not want the life of an official history painter. He worked mostly for private patrons, whose taste turned him towards scenes of love and pleasure such as The Swing and Le Verrou. In 1769 he married the miniature painter Marie-Anne Gérard, also from Grasse. Her younger sister, Marguerite Gérard, became his pupil and assistant in 1778, and their son Alexandre-Évariste, born in 1780, also became an artist.

The French Revolution cost him his patrons, who were executed or exiled. In 1790 he left Paris for Grasse and stayed with his cousin Alexandre Maubert, whose house he decorated with the Progress of Love panels he had first painted for Madame du Barry. In 1793 he joined the Commune des Arts and, with the support of Jacques-Louis David, was named one of the curators of the Louvre museum. He died in Paris on 22 August 1806, largely forgotten. Today his work is held by the Louvre, the Wallace Collection in London, the Frick Collection in New York and the National Gallery of Art in Washington, among many others.

========================================
STYLE AND SUBJECTS
========================================
Fragonard painted with speed and ease. His brushwork is loose and confident, his colour soft and warm, and his figures are caught in movement: drapery swirling, a swing at the top of its arc, lovers glancing over a shoulder. He is best known for scenes of love and flirtation, often with a hint of mischief, set in overgrown parks or private rooms. But he worked across many kinds of painting: history and religious scenes, mythology, landscapes inspired by Dutch painters, family scenes and quickly painted "fantasy figures" in costume. More than 550 of his paintings are known, though only five are dated.

The collection reflects that range. Alongside The Swing, Le Verrou and Young Girl Reading there are Italian garden views such as The Grand Staircase of the Villa d'Este at Tivoli, costumed figures like The Warrior and the Abbé de Saint-Non dressed in Spanish style, and family scenes such as The Happy Family and The Joys of Motherhood. Many prints come from his drawings and etchings: a set of satyrs and nymphs from 1763, nearly twenty illustrations to Ariosto's poem Orlando Furioso, and scenes from Don Quixote.

DECORATING WITH FRAGONARD PRINTS
========================================
Fragonard's garden scenes, with their soft blues, greens and rose pinks, suit a living room or bedroom with room for a little romance. The Swing makes an obvious centrepiece above a sofa or bed. For something calmer, Young Girl Reading, with its warm yellow dress and quiet mood, is ideal for a reading corner, a study or the wall beside a bookcase.

The darker Italian garden views, such as The Grand Staircase of the Villa d'Este at Tivoli and The Cascade, bring depth to a hallway, dining room or study, and work well as a pair. His drawings and etchings, from the 1763 satyr scenes to the Don Quixote and Orlando Furioso illustrations, are in quiet tones of ink and chalk on paper, so they sit easily in a gallery wall or a smaller room where a full-colour painting would feel like too much.

FAQ
========================================
Q: What art movement was Jean-Honoré Fragonard part of?
A: Fragonard is one of the main painters of the French Rococo, and his late Rococo manner is known for its ease, energy and sense of pleasure. After a cool reception for some of his ambitious decorative works, he also experimented with Neoclassicism.

Q: What is Jean-Honoré Fragonard known for?
A: He is best known for intimate scenes of love and flirtation, above all The Swing in the Wallace Collection, London, and Le Verrou in the Louvre, Paris. He also painted landscapes, portraits, family scenes and religious and history pictures, and made many drawings and etchings.

Q: Where can I see Jean-Honoré Fragonard's original works?
A: Major collections include the Louvre in Paris, the Wallace Collection in London, the Frick Collection and the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York, and the National Gallery of Art in Washington. In Grasse, his home town, the Musée Jean-Honoré Fragonard is in his cousin's villa, where he stayed during the Revolution.

Q: How can I recognise a Fragonard painting?
A: Look for fast, loose brushwork, tender colour and figures full of movement, with swirling drapery and expressive gestures. Many scenes are set in overgrown parks with tall trees, statues and fountains, like the Italian gardens he sketched as a young man.

Q: Is Jean-Honoré Fragonard the same person as Honoré Fragonard?
A: No. Honoré Fragonard was his cousin, an anatomist whose anatomical figures are kept at the Musée Fragonard of the veterinary school in Maisons-Alfort, near Paris. Jean-Honoré was the painter.
