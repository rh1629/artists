========================================
ABOUT LÉON SPILLIAERT
========================================
Léon Spilliaert was born in Ostend on 28 July 1881, the eldest of seven children of a hairdresser and perfumer. A sickly, solitary boy, he drew constantly and read Nietzsche and Schopenhauer at school. In 1899 he enrolled at the Bruges Academy as a pupil of Pieter Raoux, but he left within months, and he is generally regarded as self-taught.

From 1902 to 1904 he worked in Brussels for Edmond Deman, a publisher of Belgian symbolist writers, and made drawings for the three volumes of Maurice Maeterlinck's Théâtre. In 1904 he went to Paris, where the poet Emile Verhaeren became a close friend, bought his work and introduced him to dealers, among them Clovis Sagot. Back in Ostend, he walked the beaches and the seafront at night and made a remarkable series of self-portraits. From 1908 the painter Constant Permeke was a close friend.

Between 1907 and 1913 he showed at salons in Brussels, including the Salon des Indépendants. In 1916 he married Rachel Vergison, and their daughter Madeleine was born in 1917. The family lived in Brussels for a few years before returning to Ostend in the early 1920s. He showed at the Venice Biennale in 1920 and 1922, and in 1922 the Centaure gallery in Brussels gave him his first solo exhibition. The same year he was made a Knight of the Order of the Crown.

In 1935 he settled in Brussels for good, and the Palais des Beaux-Arts held retrospectives of his work in 1936 and 1944. He died in Ixelles, Brussels, on 23 November 1946. Mu.ZEE in Ostend holds the largest collection of his work, and he is also represented at the Royal Museums of Fine Arts of Belgium, the museums of fine arts in Ghent and Antwerp, the Musée d'Orsay in Paris and the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York.

========================================
STYLE AND SUBJECTS
========================================
Spilliaert worked mostly on paper, in Indian ink wash, watercolour, gouache, pastel and coloured pencil, often combined. His early work is dark and spare: deep blacks, simple shapes and wide empty spaces, with a single figure set against the sea, the seafront or a lit façade at night. He drew on Edvard Munch, Fernand Khnopff and Odilon Redon, and on writers such as Edgar Allan Poe and Nietzsche, and his mood of silence and solitude has been compared to Edward Hopper. After his marriage the work grew calmer, with brighter colour, landscapes, series of trees and a great deal of book illustration.

The collection follows that whole arc. There are the early self-portraits and night scenes, Ostend's harbour, dunes and fishermen's wives on the quay, and girls on the beach. There are dolls, interiors and still lifes, portraits of friends such as Emile Verhaeren and Constant Permeke, and the airship Belgique II in its hangar. Later work includes winter landscapes and tree studies, and full sets of lithographs: Les Serres chaudes after Maeterlinck, Sites brabançons, the 1918 winter scenes of children sledging and building snowmen, and the drawings for the 1931 children's book Au temps que Nanette était perdue.

========================================
DECORATING WITH SPILLIAERT PRINTS
========================================
Spilliaert's night seascapes and beach scenes, in deep blues, greys and blacks, suit a calm bedroom, a study or a reading corner. Strand met maan, with its moon over a dark sea, or Woman by the Sea, a lone figure facing the water, work well on their own above a bed or a desk. His studies of bare branches, in soft blue-greens, make a quiet choice for a hallway or a bathroom.

For a stronger graphic statement, the black and white spiral of Vertigo or the early self-portraits suit a modern living room or a home office with plain walls. The 1918 winter scenes of children sledging and building snowmen look good hung as a group in a child's room or a hallway, and Meisjes met witte kousjes, with its girls in blue coats and white stockings, brings warm colour to a dining room or landing.

FAQ
========================================
Q: What art movement was Léon Spilliaert part of?
A: Spilliaert is associated with Symbolism and also linked to Expressionism. Through the publisher Edmond Deman he moved among Belgian symbolist writers such as Maurice Maeterlinck and Emile Verhaeren, but he worked as an individualist rather than in one school.

Q: What is Léon Spilliaert known for?
A: He is best known for his early work, made before the First World War: dark, introspective self-portraits and night views of Ostend's beach and seafront, often with a single figure. Vertigo and Digue la nuit, both from 1908, are among his most famous images.

Q: Where can I see Léon Spilliaert's original works?
A: Mu.ZEE in Ostend has the largest collection, with more than 120 works. His work is also held by the Royal Museums of Fine Arts of Belgium in Brussels, the museums of fine arts in Ghent and Antwerp, the Musée d'Orsay in Paris and the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York.

Q: How can I recognise Léon Spilliaert's style?
A: Look for deep blacks and ink washes, simple flat shapes and large empty spaces, often with a lone figure on a beach, a seafront or a staircase at night. The mood is quiet, melancholy and slightly strange.

Q: Was Léon Spilliaert self-taught?
A: Largely, yes. He enrolled at the Bruges Academy in 1899 but left within months, and he learned mainly by drawing on his own and studying other artists' work in Brussels.
