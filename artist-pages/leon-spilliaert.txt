ABOUT LÉON SPILLIAERT
========================================
Léon Spilliaert was born in Ostend on 28 July 1881, the eldest of seven children. His father ran a hairdresser's and perfumery in the town and created his own scents, such as Brise d'Ostende. Spilliaert was largely self-taught. He enrolled at the Bruges Academy in 1899 as a pupil of Pieter Raoux, but left in January 1900, and that year he visited the Paris World Fair with his father.

From 1902 he worked for the Brussels publisher Edmond Deman, illustrating books by the symbolist writers Maurice Maeterlinck and Emile Verhaeren. In 1904 he spent time in Paris, where Verhaeren became a close friend and introduced him to dealers, including Clovis Sagot. Back in Ostend, 1907 and 1908 were his most productive years. Troubled by insomnia, he walked the seawall and the empty beach at night, and he made a striking series of self-portraits. In 1908 and 1909 he shared a studio with the painter Constant Permeke, and in 1909 the writer Stefan Zweig bought four of his works.

In 1916 he married Rachel Vergison, and in 1917 the couple moved to Brussels, where their daughter Madeleine was born. His work turned towards calmer, domestic scenes, and he made lithograph series such as Sites brabançons (1919). He showed at the Venice Biennale in 1920 and 1922, and in 1922 the Brussels gallery Le Centaure gave him his first major solo exhibition. In the early 1920s the family returned to Ostend, and in 1932 a state travel grant took them through Italy, Switzerland and Austria.

In 1935 the family settled in Brussels again, and in 1936 the Palais des Beaux-Arts held a large exhibition of his work. In 1937 he joined Les Compagnons de l'Art. During the Second World War he mostly drew trees, a subject he had always loved. He died in Ixelles, Brussels, on 23 November 1946 and is buried in Ostend. Mu.ZEE in Ostend holds the largest collection of his work, and the Musée d'Orsay in Paris also has important pieces.

========================================
STYLE AND SUBJECTS
========================================
Spilliaert worked mostly on paper, combining Indian ink wash, watercolour, gouache, pastel and coloured pencil, and he made only around 55 oil paintings. His early work uses a small, sombre palette of black, sepia and dark blue. Wide, empty beaches are cut by a firm horizon line, lamps glow on the seawall at night, and figures are often seen in silhouette or from behind. His self-portraits from 1907 and 1908 show a pale face with shadowed eyes, and his 1908 Self-Portrait with Mirror and Vertigo, both at Mu.ZEE, are among his best-known works. After his marriage the mood softened. In the 1920s he mixed casein with gouache for brighter, almost abstract seascapes, and his late tree drawings are built up from tiny strokes of ink over watercolour.

The collection follows this whole journey. There are dark seascapes and moonlit beaches, self-portraits, fishermen's wives on the Ostend quay, and quiet family interiors from 1917. A group of snowy children's scenes from 1918 shows snowball fights, sledging and a Christmas tree. There are prints titled after his lithograph series Les Serres Chaudes and Sites Brabançons, illustrations for the children's book Au temps que Nanette était perdue, and portraits of friends such as Emile Verhaeren, Henri Vandeputte and Adolphe Van Glabbeke. Later landscapes include Arbres hivernaux (1942).

DECORATING WITH SPILLIAERT PRINTS
========================================
Spilliaert's night seascapes, in deep blues, greys and black, suit calm rooms where you want quiet rather than colour: a bedroom, a study or a reading corner. Strand met maan, a moon over a dark beach, or Zwarte marine work well alone on a pale wall or as a pair, and they sit comfortably with modern, minimal furniture. His winter trees and the soft Les Serres Chaudes lithographs make a gentle, restful group for a hallway or landing.

For more colour, Brise d'Ostende. Parfum exquis, with its silhouetted woman by the blue sea, suits a bathroom or dressing room. The bright Meisjes met witte kousjes and the snowy skating and snowball scenes bring warmth to a living room, and the light pen drawings for Au temps que Nanette était perdue are a natural fit for a child's room.

FAQ
========================================
Q: What art movement was Léon Spilliaert part of?
A: Spilliaert is usually linked with Symbolism, and he moved among Belgian symbolist writers such as Maurice Maeterlinck and Emile Verhaeren. His personal style is hard to place in one movement, and some sources also connect him with Expressionism.

Q: What is Léon Spilliaert known for?
A: He is best known for moody ink-wash views of Ostend's seawall and beach at night, and for an intense series of self-portraits made in 1907 and 1908. Vertigo (1908), a woman seated on pale stairs above a dark drop, is one of his most famous images.

Q: Where can I see Léon Spilliaert's original works?
A: Mu.ZEE in Ostend holds the largest collection of his work, more than 120 pieces, including Vertigo. The Musée d'Orsay in Paris holds Digue la nuit and Clair de lune et lumières, and other works are in the Royal Museums of Fine Arts of Belgium in Brussels, the Museum of Fine Arts in Ghent, the Royal Museum of Fine Arts Antwerp and the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York.

Q: How can I recognise Léon Spilliaert's style?
A: Look for deep blacks and blues, wide empty spaces divided by a strong horizon, and lone figures shown in silhouette or from behind. Most of his work is ink wash with watercolour, pastel or coloured pencil on paper rather than oil on canvas.

Q: Did Léon Spilliaert know James Ensor?
A: Yes. Both were Ostend artists who went to the same school, twenty years apart. In 1919 Spilliaert published a text describing his friendship with and admiration for Ensor.
