ABOUT HENRI-GABRIEL IBELS
========================================
Henri-Gabriel Ibels was born in Paris on 30 November 1867 and died there in 1936. He studied at the Académie Julian, where he met Pierre Bonnard, Édouard Vuillard, Maurice Denis, Paul Ranson and Paul Sérusier. Together they formed the Nabis, a group named after the Hebrew word for prophet, and Ibels was a member from its founding in 1889.

In 1891 he showed at the Salon des Indépendants and with the Nabis at the gallery of Le Barc de Boutteville. He drew so much for newspapers and magazines that he became known as the "Nabi journaliste". From 1890 his drawings appeared in Le Père peinard, La Plume, L'Escarmouche, La Revue blanche, Le Courrier français and L'Assiette au beurre, some of them alongside his brother André, an anarchist editor. For the 1892–93 season he designed programmes for André Antoine's Théâtre Libre, and in 1893 he and his friend Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec made the lithographs for an album called Le Café-Concert.

Ibels held strong political views and was a committed Dreyfusard. During the Dreyfus affair he was behind Le Sifflet, a satirical paper created to defend Alfred Dreyfus, and his drawings for the press made him one of the best-known artists on that side of the debate. Late in life he summed up his career in his memoirs with the words "Mon arme : le crayon" (My weapon: the pencil).

He went on to work with Antoine in the theatre, taught the history of art and of costume, and ran the Ibels workshop for theatre costumes and fancy dress at the Printemps department store. He also illustrated Octave Mirbeau's novel Sébastien Roch in 1906 and served as a town councillor in Poissy. His son Robert was killed at Verdun in 1917. Long overlooked, Ibels was given his first major retrospective in 2025–26 at the Musée Maurice Denis in Saint-Germain-en-Laye and the Musée Toulouse-Lautrec in Albi. His work is held by the Art Institute of Chicago, the Metropolitan Museum of Art, the Museum of Fine Arts, Houston, the Musée Carnavalet in Paris and the Musée des Beaux-Arts in Brest.

========================================
STYLE AND SUBJECTS
========================================
Ibels drew with strong, clear outlines and simple areas of colour, in the decorative way the Nabis learned partly from Japanese prints. In 1891 the critic Félix Fénéon described his brushwork as "circumscribed by strong lines". His subjects came from modern Paris: cafés-concerts, boxing rings, the circus, the street, and the working people he met in bistros. His lithographs tell stories with bold lines and rich textures, and some, like the Théâtre Libre programme for Mirages, use brush and spatter to give a soft, speckled tone.

The collection follows these subjects closely. There are the Théâtre Libre programmes of 1892–93, with titles such as Les Tisserands, Le Devoir and Mirages, and song-sheet covers from 1895 for songs like La Chanson du Macchabée and La Valse des Bas Noirs. Circus clowns, Pierrots and fairground wrestlers sit beside posters for the Salon des Cent, the Galerie Pierrefort and his own exhibition at La Bodinière. Quieter drawings of couples, mothers and street life date from the later 1890s, and a group of 1923 costume designs for Pénélope shows his later theatre work.

DECORATING WITH IBELS PRINTS
========================================
Ibels's theatre programmes and song-sheet covers were made to catch the eye, with bold outlines and flat colour, so they hold their own on a busy wall. They suit a hallway, a home bar or a living room with a piano or record player. A Théâtre Libre programme such as Les Tisserands sits well beside a café-concert cover like La Valse des Bas Noirs, and because the 1892–93 programmes belong to one series, three or four make a natural gallery wall.

The circus and fairground scenes, such as Lutteurs de foire, bring energy to a study, a games room or a home gym. For a calmer room, the drawings of couples, mothers and women at their toilette from the later 1890s suit a bedroom or dressing area, and the 1923 Pénélope costume designs are a natural choice for a dressing room, sewing room or boutique.

FAQ
========================================
Q: What art movement was Henri-Gabriel Ibels part of?
A: Ibels was a founding member of the Nabis, the Post-Impressionist group formed in 1889 by students of the Académie Julian, including Pierre Bonnard, Édouard Vuillard, Maurice Denis and Paul Sérusier.

Q: What is Henri-Gabriel Ibels known for?
A: He is best known for lithographs, posters and press drawings of Paris theatre, café-concert and circus life in the 1890s, especially his programmes for the Théâtre Libre. His constant work for newspapers, including his drawings during the Dreyfus affair, earned him the nickname "Nabi journaliste".

Q: Did Henri-Gabriel Ibels work with Toulouse-Lautrec?
A: Yes. The two were friends, and in 1893 they made the lithographs for Le Café-Concert, an album with text by Georges Montorgueil celebrating the singers and performers of the Paris cafés-concerts.

Q: Where can I see Henri-Gabriel Ibels's original works?
A: In the United States, the Art Institute of Chicago, the Metropolitan Museum of Art, the Museum of Fine Arts, Houston and the RISD Museum hold his work, including Théâtre Libre programmes. In France, look for him at the Musée Maurice Denis in Saint-Germain-en-Laye, the Musée des Beaux-Arts in Brest and the Musée Carnavalet in Paris.

Q: How can I recognise Henri-Gabriel Ibels's style?
A: Look for strong, clear outlines, flat colour and lively figures from Paris entertainment and street life: actors, singers, clowns and crowds. Many of his prints are signed "H.G. Ibels" in the plate or on the stone.
