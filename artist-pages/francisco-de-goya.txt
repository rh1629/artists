ABOUT FRANCISCO DE GOYA
========================================
Francisco José de Goya y Lucientes was born on 30 March 1746 in Fuendetodos, a village in Aragon, and grew up in nearby Zaragoza, where his father worked as a gilder. As a teenager he trained in Zaragoza under the painter José Luzán. He twice entered the competitions of the Royal Academy of San Fernando in Madrid without success, and in 1770–71 he travelled to Italy at his own expense. Back in Zaragoza he painted frescoes for the Basilica of the Pillar, and in 1773 he married Josefa Bayeu, sister of the painter Francisco Bayeu.

In 1775 he moved to Madrid to design tapestry cartoons for the Royal Tapestry Factory, work then led by the court painter Anton Raphael Mengs. These Rococo scenes of popular Spanish life brought him to wider attention. He became a member of the Royal Academy in 1780, painter to the king in 1786, and court painter to Charles IV in 1789. His portraits of the nobility and the royal family, including Charles IV of Spain and His Family (1800–01), are known for their refusal to flatter.

In 1792–93 a serious illness left him permanently deaf. He recovered in Cádiz at the home of his friend Sebastián Martínez, and his work became more personal and darker. In 1795 he became director of painting at the Royal Academy, and in 1799 he was made First Court Painter, the highest rank for a Spanish court painter. The same year he published Los Caprichos, a set of 80 satirical etchings. During the Peninsular War he stayed in Madrid. He worked on the Disasters of War etchings, which were not published until 1863, and in 1814 painted The Second of May 1808 and The Third of May 1808.

After the war came La Tauromaquia, a series of bullfighting prints on sale from 1816, and the strange, dreamlike Disparates etchings. In 1819 he bought a country house outside Madrid, the Quinta del Sordo ("House of the Deaf Man"), and painted its walls with the fourteen Black Paintings. Fearing the repression of liberals after 1823, he left Spain in 1824 and settled in Bordeaux, where he kept drawing and painting until his death on 16 April 1828. Most of his work is held by the Museo del Prado in Madrid.

========================================
STYLE AND SUBJECTS
========================================
Goya's style changed greatly over his long career, moving from Rococo through Neoclassicism towards early Romanticism. What stayed constant was his eye for real life: he painted people as they were, without idealising them. His later paintings are loosely brushed, and the Black Paintings reduce the palette to ochres, earths, greys and black. In his prints he combined etching with aquatint to create soft areas of tone, and used them to mock the vanity, superstition and corruption he saw around him, from the nobility to the clergy.

The collection reflects this range. Dozens of plates from Los Caprichos sit alongside the later Disparates, with their proverb titles and night-time visions. There are around 40 portraits, among them his friends Martín Zapater and the writer Leandro Fernández de Moratín, his grandson Mariano and several self-portraits. Many drawings record everyday Spanish life: beggars, majas, monks, dancers, drinkers and hunters. There is also a six-part story of Friar Pedro and the bandit El Maragato, a group of saints, and a still life of golden bream.

DECORATING WITH GOYA PRINTS
========================================
Goya's portraits bring warmth and character to a living or dining room. Condesa de Altamira and Her Daughter, María Agustina, in soft pink and blue silk, suits a bedroom or a quiet sitting room, while the Red Boy's bright red works well in a hallway or a child's room. For a kitchen or dining room, try Still Life With Golden Bream or The Water Carrier, with its warm yellow skirt against a grey-blue sky.

The Caprichos and Disparates etchings, in black ink on cream paper, suit a study, library or hallway. They are at their best hung as a group, where their short, ironic captions can be read side by side. Darker scenes such as The Third of May 1808 and Witches' Flight make strong statement pieces for a study or a dining room, where people will want to talk about them.

FAQ
========================================
Q: What art movement was Francisco de Goya part of?
A: Goya is usually placed with Romanticism. His style moved from Rococo through Neoclassicism to an early Romantic manner, and he is often called the last of the Old Masters and the first of the moderns.

Q: What is Francisco de Goya known for?
A: He is known for his portraits of the Spanish court, his print series Los Caprichos and the Disasters of War, and paintings such as The Third of May 1808 and La maja desnuda. The Black Paintings from his house outside Madrid are among his most famous late works.

Q: Where can I see Francisco de Goya's original works?
A: The Museo del Prado in Madrid holds most of his work, including The Third of May 1808 and the Black Paintings. Other works are in the Royal Academy of San Fernando in Madrid, the Metropolitan Museum of Art and the Frick Collection in New York, and the Louvre in Paris.

Q: How can I recognise Goya's style?
A: Look for honest, unflattering faces, loose brushwork and dark backgrounds lit by sudden light. In his prints, look for soft grey aquatint tones and short, ironic captions under each scene.

Q: Is it Francisco Goya or Francisco de Goya?
A: Both are correct. His full name was Francisco José de Goya y Lucientes. Towards the end of the 1790s he changed his name from Francisco Goya to Francisco de Goya.
