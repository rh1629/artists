ABOUT AGNOLO BRONZINO
========================================
Agnolo di Cosimo, known as Bronzino, was born in Florence on 17 November 1503, the son of a butcher. His nickname may refer to his relatively dark skin or reddish hair. According to the biographer Giorgio Vasari, he trained first with Raffaellino del Garbo and then with Pontormo, the leading Florentine painter of the first generation of Mannerism.

Pontormo shaped his style, and the two worked together for much of Pontormo's life. In the mid-1520s Bronzino helped decorate the Capponi Chapel in the church of Santa Felicita, where Vasari credits him with two of the four roundels of the evangelists. His style was so close to his master's that scholars still debate which are his. In the early 1530s he worked in Pesaro for the Della Rovere family, painting frescoes at the Villa Imperiale alongside the Dossi brothers.

In 1539 Bronzino was one of the artists chosen to create the decorations for the wedding of Cosimo I de' Medici and Eleonora di Toledo. He soon became the duke's court painter, a post he held for most of his career. He portrayed the duke, the duchess, their children and their court, decorated Eleonora's private chapel in the Palazzo Vecchio, and designed tapestries on the story of Joseph. Cosimo sent copied portraits as diplomatic gifts, so many of these pictures exist in several versions.

Bronzino was also a poet, and his friends included the poet Laura Battiferri. After Pontormo's death in 1557 he finished his master's frescoes in the church of San Lorenzo, and in 1563 he took a leading part in the new Accademia delle Arti del Disegno. He died in Florence on 23 November 1572, in the home of his favourite pupil, Alessandro Allori. Many of his paintings remain in Florence, and others hang in London, Paris, Milan and New York.

========================================
STYLE AND SUBJECTS
========================================
Bronzino was above all a portraitist. His sitters are elegant, self-assured and reserved, and he paid minute attention to their clothes: in his portrait of Eleonora di Toledo with her son, the patterned gown takes up more of the picture than either sitter. His figures are graceful and slightly elongated in the Mannerist way, but calmer than those of his teacher Pontormo. In religious and mythological scenes he borrowed poses from Michelangelo and Raphael, and his skill with the nude is at its most striking in Venus, Cupid, Folly and Time.

The collection reflects this range. There are court portraits of Cosimo I, Eleonora di Toledo and Bia de' Medici, of the poet Laura Battiferri, and of the admiral Andrea Doria posing as the sea god Neptune. Alongside them are Holy Family and Madonna scenes, saints such as Sebastian and John the Baptist, the Deposition and Resurrection of Christ, and mythologies including Venus, Cupid and Satyr. A small group of drawings, among them The Fall of Phaethon and studies of heads and a hand, shows his work on paper.

DECORATING WITH BRONZINO PRINTS
========================================
Bronzino's portraits have a calm formality that suits rooms with a sense of occasion: a dining room, an entrance hall or a study. Pair the deep red of Portrait of a Lady in Red with the black and green of Portrait of Lodovico Capponi, or hang Portrait of Eleanor of Toledo with Her Son Giovanni de' Medici above a sideboard or fireplace as a single centrepiece.

The Holy Family and Madonna scenes, with their soft blues, rose reds and gentle faces, suit a bedroom or a quiet reading corner. Venus, Cupid, Folly and Time is a busy, vivid allegory that works best on its own in a living room. The pale drawings of heads, hands and mythological figures look good grouped together in a study or along a narrow hallway.

FAQ
========================================
Q: What art movement was Agnolo Bronzino part of?
A: Bronzino was a Mannerist painter of the late Renaissance in Florence. He trained with Pontormo, the leading Florentine painter of the first generation of Mannerism, and his style was strongly shaped by him.

Q: What is Agnolo Bronzino known for?
A: He is best known for his portraits of Cosimo I de' Medici, Eleonora di Toledo and their court, which influenced European court portraiture for a century. His best-known single painting is the allegory Venus, Cupid, Folly and Time.

Q: Where can I see Agnolo Bronzino's original works?
A: Many are still in Florence, in the Uffizi, the Palazzo Vecchio and the Galleria dell'Accademia. Venus, Cupid, Folly and Time is in the National Gallery, London, and other works are in the Louvre in Paris, the Pinacoteca di Brera in Milan, and the Metropolitan Museum of Art and the Frick Collection in New York.

Q: How can I recognise Bronzino's style?
A: Look for calm, elegant, slightly elongated figures with composed, reserved expressions, and for close attention to the detail of clothes and fabrics. His portrait sitters often seem self-assured and a little distant.

Q: Is Agnolo Bronzino the same artist as Alessandro Allori?
A: No. Alessandro Allori (1535–1607) was Bronzino's favourite pupil, brought up and trained by him after the death of Allori's father. Allori and his son Cristofano sometimes used the name "Bronzino", which can cause confusion.
